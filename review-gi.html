GI Anatomy and Physiology

For further study, see Fundamentals II Week 3 – Nutrition.

The GI system's three main functions: transportation, digestion and absorption.

The Path Through the GI Tract

  1. Esophagus, then the esophageal sphincter.
  2. Stomach: a very acidic environment.
  3. Pyloric sphincter.
  4. Small intestine: duodenum, jejunum, ileum. The duodenum sits just past the pyloric sphincter, the ileum just before the large intestine. Nutrients are absorbed here.
  5. Large intestine (colon): ascending, transverse, descending, sigmoid. Water is reabsorbed here, not nutrients.
  6. Rectum, then anus.
PartWhat It Does
Esophagus
  • Peristalsis, waves of muscle contraction, carries food to the stomach.
  • The lower esophageal sphincter keeps stomach contents from flowing back up.
Stomach
  • Stores and churns food with hydrochloric acid and pepsin, which begin breaking down protein.
  • A mucus layer protects its lining from its own acid. Empties slowly into the duodenum.
Small intestine
  • Most digestion happens here: bile and pancreatic enzymes enter the duodenum.
  • The terminal ileum also absorbs vitamin B12 and bile salts.
Large intestine
  • Absorbs electrolytes along with the water, and forms and stores stool.
Liver
  • The portal vein brings it the blood from the stomach, intestines, pancreas and spleen. The liver processes what was absorbed before it reaches the rest of the body.
  • Makes bile, which digests fat.
Gallbladder
  • Stores and concentrates bile, and releases it into the duodenum after a fatty meal.
Pancreas
  • Exocrine: releases amylase (carbohydrates), lipase (fats) and protease (proteins) into the duodenum, with bicarbonate to neutralize the stomach acid.
  • Endocrine: releases insulin and glucagon into the blood.

Inflammatory Bowel Disease

For further study, see Med Surg I Week 6 – Inflammatory Bowel Disease.

What Crohn's Disease and Ulcerative Colitis Share

  • Chronic inflammation of the intestine, with periods of remission and exacerbation (flares).
  • Cause unknown. Autoimmune. No cure, except removing the whole colon in ulcerative colitis.
  • Signs and symptoms: diarrhea, weight loss, abdominal pain, fever, fatigue.
  • Complications: hemorrhage, strictures, perforation. Sometimes surgery.
  • Treatment: medication to decrease the inflammation and maintain remission, and surgery.

Crohn's Disease

  • Where: anywhere from mouth to anus. Most often the terminal ileum and the colon.
  • Pattern: skip lesions, irregular. Deep ulcerations with a cobblestone appearance.
  • Depth: transmural.
  • Rectal bleeding: sometimes.
  • Malabsorption and nutritional deficiencies: common. More of a small intestine disease.
  • Complications: perianal abscesses and fistulas, strictures, perforation: the inflammation involves the entire bowel wall.
  • Cancer risk: increased for small intestinal cancer. Colorectal too, but less than with ulcerative colitis.
  • Surgery: for complications such as a stricture.

Ulcerative Colitis

  • Where: the large intestine only. Starts in the rectum and spreads up the colon.
  • Pattern: uniform and continuous.
  • Depth: submucosa or mucosa. Not as deep.
  • Rectal bleeding: common.
  • Malabsorption and nutritional deficiencies: minimal. The colon absorbs water, not nutrients.
  • Complications: perforation, from toxic megacolon.
  • Cancer risk: increased colorectal cancer after 10 years of disease.
  • Surgery: curative. With the colon out, the disease is gone.

Goals of Treatment in a Flare

  • Rest the bowel: NPO, with IV fluids to hydrate.
  • Control the inflammation, with medication.
  • Combat infection, if any.
  • Correct malnutrition: severe disease often leaves the patient exceptionally malnourished.
  • Alleviate stress: stress makes it worse.
  • Relieve symptoms and improve quality of life, with drug therapy.

Irritable Bowel Syndrome

Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)

  • A functional bowel disorder: chronic abdominal pain with a change in bowel habits, and no visible inflammation or damage to the bowel.
  • Not IBD: no inflammation, ulceration or bleeding, and no added risk of colon cancer.
  • Types, by the main stool pattern: constipation (IBS-C), diarrhea (IBS-D), or mixed (IBS-M).
  • Signs: cramping abdominal pain, often eased by a bowel movement. Bloating, gas, mucus in the stool. Often triggered by certain foods or stress.
  • Red flags point elsewhere, such as to IBD or cancer: rectal bleeding, weight loss, anemia, fever, onset after age 50.
  • Managed with diet changes, stress management, and drugs for the main symptom.

Peptic Ulcer Disease

For further study, see Med Surg I Week 6 – Peptic Ulcer Disease.

Peptic ulcer disease (PUD): erosion of the GI mucosa from the digestive action of hydrochloric acid and pepsin.

Causes of PUD

  • H. pylori: triggers inflammation.
  • Drugs: NSAIDs above all, such as ibuprofen and naproxen.
  • H. pylori with NSAIDs: double trouble, a greater risk of an ulcer.
  • Also: alcohol, smoking, family history.
  • Stress aggravates an existing ulcer. Not shown to cause one.
UlcerWhat to Know
GastricMost often in the antrum of the stomach.
DuodenalJust past the pyloric sphincter. 80% of ulcers. 90 to 95% have H. pylori.
AcuteThrough fewer layers of the wall. Short duration: resolves quickly.
Chronic4 times more common. Deeper, more severe and long-term.

Diagnosing PUD

  • EGD: the most accurate diagnostic procedure.
  • H. pylori, non-invasive: a urea breath test and a stool test. Urea is a byproduct of the bacteria's metabolism.
  • H. pylori, invasive: biopsy of the antral mucosa, tested for urease. The gold standard.
  • CBC: the H&H, for anemia. Is it bleeding, or has it been?
  • Stool for occult blood. See Upper GI Bleed below.

Complications of PUD

The complications of PUD: HOP.
  • HOP: Hemorrhage, Obstruction, Perforation.
ComplicationWhat to Know
Hemorrhage
  • The most common complication.
  • Watch the vital signs' trend: a heart rate creeping up through the day.
  • Gastric aspirate: a change in its amount or redness. Anything attached to the patient is part of the assessment.
  • Pain may decrease at first: the blood neutralizes the gastric acid.
  • NG to low wall suction, to decompress the stomach. Otherwise as for an upper GI bleed, below.
Obstruction
  • Can happen at any time. Most likely with an ulcer close to the pylorus.
  • Gradual onset of symptoms.
  • NG to low wall suction until it resolves, irrigated per protocol.
Perforation
  • The most lethal complication: gastric contents reach the peritoneum.
  • Sudden and dramatic. In the first 2 hours: sudden, severe upper abdominal pain, spreading throughout the abdomen, radiating to the back, not relieved by food or antacids.
  • Rigid, board-like abdomen. Rebound tenderness.
  • Respirations shallow and rapid. Pulse weak and tachycardic. Bowel sounds may be absent, after listening 5 minutes.
  • Bacterial peritonitis within 6 to 12 hours.

Collaborative Care for PUD

  • Adequate rest: lowers the stress response.
  • Smoking cessation.
  • Diet: no special diet. Individualized.
  • Stop aspirin and NSAIDs for 4 to 6 weeks. If aspirin must continue, give it with a PPI.
  • Drugs, a vital part of treatment: antibiotics if H. pylori, a PPI (omeprazole, esomeprazole), and sucralfate, a cytoprotective agent.

Upper GI Bleed

For further study, see Med Surg I Week 6 – Upper Gastrointestinal Bleed.

An upper GI bleed's onset: slow and insidious, or sudden.

SignWhat It Means
Bright red hematemesisBlood in the vomit, seen with the naked eye ("frank" or "gross"): a recent or ongoing bleed.
Coffee-ground hematemesisThe blood has been partly digested: an older bleed, or a significant bleed has stopped. Very malodorous.
MelenaBlack, tarry, malodorous stool: a transit time over 8 hours. Helps the provider find where the bleed began.
Occult bloodNot apparent by appearance. Found by a positive guaiac test, never an expected finding.

Causes of an Upper GI Bleed

  • Stomach and duodenal ulcers: about 50% of upper GI bleeds. H. pylori and NSAIDs are the two most common causes of a bleeding ulcer.
  • Drug-induced ulcers: NSAIDs, salicylates such as aspirin, steroids. Many are over the counter.
  • Stress-related mucosal disorder (SRMD): a mucosal injury in 70 to 90% of ICU patients. So ICU patients are typically on a PPI, IV pantoprazole.
  • Esophageal varices, from portal hypertension. See Liver Failure below.
InterventionWhat to Know
Vital signs
  • Closely: the baseline, and the trend.
  • Orthostatic hypotension points to hypovolemia.
Signs of hypovolemia
  • Rapid blood loss can bring hypovolemic shock.
  • A rising heart rate, increased thirst, cold clammy skin, restlessness. Always investigate restlessness.
Abdomen
  • Tense, rigid, board-like: a gut perforation or peritonitis, with distension and guarding.
Labs
  • H&H and BUN. An elevated BUN is not always the kidneys: a GI bleed raises it too.
IV access
  • Large bore, 18-gauge preferred. Anticipate PRBCs, and at least fluid resuscitation.
NG lavage
  • Washes the blood out of the stomach. Effectiveness questionable.
Emesis and stool
  • Assess them, and check for occult blood.
Endoscopy
  • Hemostasis is the first-line management: the provider finds the bleed and stops it, as by cautery. No other intervention helps until then.

Chronic Pancreatitis

For further study, see Med Surg I Week 7 – Pancreatitis.

Chronic pancreatitis: progressive and destructive, with remissions and flares. Caused by inflammation and fibrosis of the pancreatic tissue.

Signs of Chronic Pancreatitis

  • Intense abdominal pain. Less tenderness than in acute pancreatitis.
  • A mass: suspect a pseudocyst, or an abscess within the pancreas that needs prompt surgical drainage.
  • Ascites, with respiratory compromise from it.
  • Steatorrhea: the pancreatic enzymes are not working.
  • Dark urine: from increased bilirubin. The whole hepatobiliary tree is interconnected.
CareWhat to Know
Pain
  • Opioids are used, cautiously: some may increase the pain by causing spasms.
Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT)
  • The standard of care, to prevent malnutrition, malabsorption and excessive weight loss.
  • Usually pancrelipase: amylase, lipase and protease.
  • Record the number and consistency of stools each day. Goal: fewer, and less fatty.
Giving the enzymes
  • With the first bite of every meal and snack, when the body would secrete them.
  • Swallowed whole, not chewed. Wipe any off the lips: enzymes break things down.
  • Not mixed into protein foods. Enteric-coated forms not crushed.
  • Sometimes with an antacid or H2 blocker: a low pH inactivates them.
Nutrition
  • Weight loss can be significant. Sometimes a candidate for TPN.
  • Eating by mouth: may need 4,000 to 6,000 calories a day to maintain weight.
Preventing exacerbations
  • Avoid what makes the symptoms worse: alcohol, with a referral to a self-help group, caffeinated drinks, nicotine.
  • Meals: bland, low-fat, high-protein, high-carbohydrate. No gastric stimulants, such as spices. Small, high-calorie meals and snacks.
  • Rest frequently until strength returns.

Liver Failure: Cirrhosis

For further study, see Med Surg I Week 7 – Cirrhosis.

What Is Going On in the Liver

Early Cirrhosis

  • Insidious onset.
  • Weight loss and weakness.
  • GI disturbances: anorexia, nausea and vomiting, flatulence, a change in bowel habits.
  • Hepatomegaly, with RUQ pain and a palpable liver.

Late Cirrhosis

  • Jaundice: often the first sign.
  • Low albumin, a protein the liver makes. Albumin has pulling power: without it, fluid shifts out of the vessels into the interstitium. Third spacing, and ascites.
  • Low prothrombin, a protein the liver makes and a precursor in the clotting cascade. Bleeding times increase, and so does the risk of bleeding.
  • Portal hypertension and its complications: see below.
  • Hepatic encephalopathy, with asterixis: see below.

Portal Hypertension and Its Complications

Portal hypertension: the blood coming into the liver through the portal system backs up, from congestion in the cirrhotic liver, as in heart failure. The pressure in the portal area rises, and the backed-up fluid engorges vessels.

ComplicationWhat to Know
Ascites
  • Fluid backs up into the peritoneum: third spacing.
  • Low albumin aggravates it. Managing it: see below.
Splenomegaly
  • The congestion enlarges the spleen, in the left upper quadrant.
  • The spleen filters the blood, removing microbes and worn-out RBCs, and makes WBCs and antibodies.
Spider angiomas and caput medusae
  • Engorged vessels.
  • Caput medusae: dilated veins on the abdomen, like varicose veins.
Esophageal and anorectal varices
  • Engorged veins, like hemorrhoids. The esophagus sits close to the liver.
  • Bleeding varices are very dangerous: the patient already lacks prothrombin. Prevent them, and keep them from bleeding.
Ascites, splenomegaly, varices, engorged vessels: all from the portal hypertension.

Managing Ascites

MeasureWhat to Know
Abdominal girth
  • Measured and trended. Daily weights are a more accurate indicator of fluid retention.
Sodium and fluid restriction
  • Water follows salt: a low-sodium diet, and possibly a fluid restriction.
Diuretics
  • Spironolactone, potassium-sparing, and a loop diuretic such as furosemide, potassium-losing. Often both.
  • Assumes the kidneys are working well.
Paracentesis
  • Goal: relieve respiratory distress, once the ascites impairs breathing.
  • Often repeated in cirrhosis: large-volume paracentesis.
TIPS (transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt)
  • Non-surgical. A stent lowers the portal hypertension: controls long-term ascites and reduces variceal bleeding.
IV albumin
  • Raises the oncotic pressure: more pulling power into the vascular space.

Hepatic Encephalopathy

For further study, see Med Surg I Week 7 – Cirrhosis.

What Causes Hepatic Encephalopathy

  • A reversible neuropsychiatric manifestation of impaired liver function.
  • Primarily too much serum ammonia. Most ammonia forms when bacteria in the intestines break down protein.
  • A working liver converts ammonia to urea, excreted in the urine. A failing liver does not.
  • Ammonia crosses the blood-brain barrier: a CNS depressant, with a sedative effect.

What Rising Ammonia Does

  • Consciousness: slowed responses and confusion, then lethargy, semi-stupor, and hepatic coma.
  • Intellect: confusion, loss of place, inability to compute, loss of self, then no intellect.
  • Personality: depression, irritability, decreased inhibitions, then no personality or behavior at all.
  • Neuromuscular: asterixis, poor coordination, impaired handwriting, ataxia, then coma.
The goal: get the ammonia down, and keep the patient safe. Without that, the patient ends up comatose.
MeasureWhy
Protein
  • Restricted only with severe encephalopathy, at end stage: 20 to 40 g a day.
  • Otherwise its benefits outweigh the ammonia its breakdown adds: the lesser of two evils.
  • Calorie-dense food, especially carbohydrates.
Control GI bleeding
  • Blood in the GI tract is nitrogen-rich, another source of protein: it drives up the ammonia.
Avoid constipation
  • Constipation increases the ammonia in the feces.
Lactulose
  • The bowel movements remove the ammonia.
  • Teach why: a patient who has already had a bowel movement today still needs the dose.
Neuro checks and safety
  • EMV (the Glasgow Coma Scale) regularly, for the sedative effect of the ammonia.
  • Safety precautions: these patients are often confused.

How Age Affects the GI System

For further study, see Fundamentals II Week 6 – Older Adults and Fundamentals II Week 4 – Bowel.

WhereWhat Changes With Age
Mouth, taste and smell
  • Trouble chewing. Less saliva: a drier mouth.
  • Taste buds atrophy and lose sensitivity: harder to tell salt, sweet, bitter and sour apart. Health conditions and medications can alter taste.
  • Loss of smell. With taste and smell dwindling, nutrition is a challenge.
Esophagus
  • Emptying slows.
Stomach
  • Less hydrochloric acid, which starts breaking down the food.
  • Delayed gastric emptying: not hungry as quickly.
  • Less tolerant of foods.
Intestines
  • Slower peristalsis.
  • Impaired absorption, of vitamins too.
  • Fewer digestive enzymes, including lipase for fat digestion.
Rectum and anus
  • Weakened sphincters.
  • Less sensation to defecate.
Abdomen
  • More fatty tissue in the trunk and abdomen: more protuberant.

Why Older Adults Get Constipated

  • Lack of muscle tone, in the bowel and the abdomen.
  • Slowed peristalsis: more time for water to be reabsorbed.
  • Lack of exercise, too little fluid, too little fiber.
  • Too many dairy products, such as cheese.
  • Medications.
