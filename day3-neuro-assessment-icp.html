Neurologic Assessment

The most important focused assessment: neurologic function controls so many of the other body systems. Two parts: brain function and spinal function.

AssessmentWhat to Know
Level of consciousness
  • The first thing, on walking into the room.
  • Alert to comatose, or paralyzed and sedated.
Glasgow Coma Scale
  • Takes the level of consciousness to the next level: eyes, motor and verbal responses. See below.
Sensory and motor function
  • Shows brain function and spinal function.
Memory and reflexes
  • Higher levels of neurologic function.
  • Memory: not tested on every visit, but often by neuro ICU and stroke nurses.
  • Reflexes: show spinal and brain function. Tested a lot when brain function may be decreased or the brain injured.
Respiratory functioning
  • Neurologic status controls and maintains the airway, above all.
  • Drowsy, close to obtunded, not responding to voice: can the patient maintain the airway?
  • Breathing patterns: see below.

The levels of consciousness and the focused neuro assessment are taught in the Neuro review

Know the neurologic baseline. Gentle, nuanced changes can mean changes in the brain's arterial supply and perfusion. Get the history: a patient who takes a sleeping pill may normally wake confused.

Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)

Best Motor ResponseAt the Bedside
Obeys (6)Squeezes the hands, wiggles the toes, on a verbal command.
Localizes pain (5)Shakes the hand when the nail bed is squeezed.
Flexion-withdrawal (4)Moves the hand a little.

Total: 3 to 15. The lowest GCS is 3.

The full Glasgow Coma Scale, eyes, motor and verbal, is taught in the Neuro review

Scoring the Sedated or Intubated Patient

  • Paralyzed and sedated on purpose: GCS 3. Comment in the GCS box that the patient is paralyzed and sedated.
  • Sedation vacation (sedation break): sedation titrated down or paused, usually once a day, for a neuro status. Neurologic issues can be hidden in paralyzed and sedated patients.
  • Intubated or trached: no verbal response, so the score carries a T: 10T, 11T. Eyes open and following commands, but not talking.
  • Baseline: dementia can lower the GCS. Disoriented but conversing: GCS 14.

GCS scores and airway protection are taught in Respiratory and Progressive Issues in Critical Care

Posturing

Decorticate Posturing

  • Muscles: all turn in.
  • Arms: bent toward the center of the body. Curled wrists, balled hands against the chest.
  • Legs: rigid and extended. Toes pointed and turned in.
  • GCS motor score: 3, flexion-abnormal.

Decerebrate Posturing

  • Muscles: all turn out.
  • Arms: straight, tense and parallel to the body. Flexed wrists, curled fingers.
  • Legs: rigid and extended. Toes pointed and turned in.
  • GCS motor score: 2, extension.
Decorticate is to the core. Decerebrate is away from the core. Either one indicates a significant neurological injury: inappropriate brain function.

Pupils and Breathing Patterns

Pupil Checks

  • Crucial in high-risk patients, especially the sedated, who may not obey commands, and for whom a sedation vacation may not be possible.
  • Any change is a neurologic issue: one or both pupils blown, or not reactive to light.
  • Compare with the baseline: reactive to light earlier in the night, and not now, is a big deal.
  • Time is brain cells: decompression or another intervention may help, but only once the change is noticed. Check neuro status together at bedside report.
One pupil a different size from the other is an immediate stroke alert. It can mean pressure on one side of the brain.

Breathing Patterns in Neurologic Disorders

  • Abnormal patterns show where in the brain the lesion is. All are very abnormal.
  • These patients are usually intubated, so the pattern is seen with the ventilator paused, or when care is withdrawn and the patient extubated.
  • Withdrawal of care: reassure the family the patient is comfortable. The pattern is the result of what is going on physiologically.
  • Examples: Cheyne-Stokes respiration, apneustic breathing, cluster breathing.

Increased Intracranial Pressure (ICP)

Increased.

Above 20, Over 5 Minutes

← Lower ICPHigher ICP →

Can result from: head injury, bleeding in the brain, tumors, infections, extra fluid in the brain, stroke.

Three Components of Cranial Pressure

What Makes Up Cranial Pressure

  • Cerebral tissue: the brain itself.
  • Blood: arterial and venous flow, perfusing and draining the brain.
  • Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF): held in the ventricles.
Three water balloons in a bucket. When one expands, the other two are compressed. The skull cannot give the way other areas of the body can, so the end point is herniation, which can kill the patient.

Causes of Increased ICP

CauseWhat to Know
Cerebral edema
  • Brain tissue swollen: increased brain volume.
  • Two types, with different mechanisms: cytotoxic and vasogenic.
Increased cerebral blood volume
  • Loss of autoregulation: from decreased cerebral oxygenation, high metabolic demands, or obstructed outflow.
  • More blood to the brain helps a healthy person under stress. With raised ICP, the aim is less blood volume, to cut the brain's demand and the ICP.
  • Raised in the ICU by: suctioning, turning, pain, vital sign changes, head hyperflexion or extension during oral or trach care.
Increased CSF
  • Hydrocephalus: more CSF on board, from an obstruction such as a tumor, or blocked absorption after a subarachnoid hemorrhage or infection.
  • One of the easier causes to control.

Signs of Increased ICP

Increased ICP is a medical emergency. When the ICP rises above the mean arterial pressure (MAP), the brain can no longer receive oxygen.

Vital Sign Changes

  • Cushing's triad: bradycardia, irregular respirations and a widened pulse pressure.

Other Symptoms, If Awake

  • Significant headache: one of the big ones.
  • Vomiting, blurred vision.
  • Lethargy, weakness.
  • Changes in behavior.

ICP Monitoring and Cerebral Perfusion

ICP Monitoring: What It Is For

  • Correlates objective data with the clinical picture to look at cerebral perfusion. The aim is a perfused brain and a stable neurologic status, not the number itself.
  • Usually for patients with a GCS of 3 to 8.
  • Assesses response to therapy: is the ICP decreasing after part of the skull is removed?
  • Classified by location of the device or type of transducer: epidural, ventriculostomy and others.
  • Ventricular devices are the most common: a catheter into a ventricle, connected to a drainage bag. Drains CSF in a controlled manner and monitors ICP.
  • Shows the ICP waveform.
  • Read with the other assessments of perfusion: level of consciousness, nerve function, pupil changes.

Cerebral Perfusion Pressure (CPP)

CPP = MAP − ICP. Cerebral perfusion is how much blood the brain is getting.

CPP Targets

  • Normal: 60 to 100.
  • Goal for neurological injuries: above 70, for enough blood flow to heal, bringing oxygenated, nutritious blood to the injured area.
  • Usually needs a higher MAP than normal, or the ICP kept as low as possible.
  • Example: an ICP of 20 needs a MAP of 90 for a CPP of 70.

Managing Increased ICP

Goals: ICP below 20, CPP above 70. Keep the MAP up.

Monitoring and Support for High ICP

  • Monitor and assess neurologic status: often sedated, so pupils, reflexes, posturing, response to pain and vital signs, with the ICP.
  • Maintain the airway, provide ventilation and oxygenation, decrease the brain's metabolic demands. Usually established early.
  • Usually sedated and paralyzed, to let the brain rest, with high MAPs for perfusion.
Nursing CareWhat to Know
Positioning
  • Head of bed at least 30°: promotes cerebral blood flow and CSF drainage.
  • Head neutral and midline, propped up, not sinking forward.
  • BP may drop as the head of the bed rises: a delicate balance with the CPP and ICP.
Turning
  • Evaluate how the ICP responds. Goal: back to baseline within 5 minutes.
  • Brain perfusion is more important than skin. If the ICP stays up, turn very gently.
Endotracheal suctioning
  • Only when necessary: rhonchi, not clear lungs.
  • Preoxygenate at 100%. Hypoxemia elevates ICP, and suctioning can increase it.
  • 10 seconds at a time, the whole procedure within 1 minute.
Hygiene
  • Bed baths: as with turning, the ICP back to baseline within 5 minutes.
Family
  • Family presence and gentle touch decrease ICP.
  • Teach the family to keep a calm, restorative environment, with the TV quiet.

Medical Management of Increased ICP and CPP

InterventionWhat to Know
Oxygenation and ventilation
  • PaO2 above 80, for maximum oxygen delivery to the brain. Normally 60 is acceptable.
  • PaO2 below 50 increases ICP, so these patients are typically sedated and intubated.
  • PEEP kept low: it can increase ICP. Tidal volume and FiO2 are maximized instead.
  • Hyperventilation: lowers CO2, which can help increase CPP. Once first line, now less common. Used for an immediate need, such as active herniation.
Diuretics
  • Loop and osmotic diuretics: decrease cerebral blood volume.
  • Mannitol: an osmotic diuretic, working in minutes. Watch for hypotension and electrolyte problems.
  • Stopping mannitol suddenly can cause a big increase in ICP.
  • Often given with vasopressors, to keep the MAP up while the patient diureses.
Fluid administration
  • Maintain and optimize the MAP.
  • Normal saline or hypertonic fluids at times, to avoid cerebral edema.
  • Strict I&O.
  • Serum osmolality below 320.
BP management
  • MAP usually 70 to 90: whatever keeps the CPP consistently above 70.
  • Systolic below about 160: above 160 is a higher risk of stroke.

PEEP is taught in Oxygen Therapy and Mechanical Ventilation

Vasoactive medications are taught in Shock, Sepsis, MODS

Reducing Metabolic Demands

MeasureWhat to Know
Temperature control
  • No fever: fever greatly increases metabolic demand. It can follow a TBI or spinal cord injury.
  • Cooling blankets, cooling pads, acetaminophen.
  • Targeted temperature management: cooled to about 34 to 35°C for about 24 hours, then rewarmed gradually.
Sedation
  • Reduces pain, agitation, restlessness and resistance to mechanical ventilation, which all increase ICP.
  • Sedatives can lower BP: keep the BP up.
Avoiding seizures
  • Seizures greatly increase ICP, and brain-injured patients are prone to them.
  • Often a prophylactic anti-seizure drug.
Paralytics (neuromuscular blockade)
  • A step beyond sedation.
Surgical intervention
  • Craniectomy: part of the skull taken out, to open up the bucket.
  • May be needed to address the cause of the increased ICP.

Psychosocial Impact of Traumatic Neurological Injury

Supporting the Patient and Family

  • Sudden and unexpected: no one wakes up expecting a stroke, a car accident or a fall.
  • Life-changing: may be paralyzed for good, or have lifelong or temporary neurologic impairment.
  • Support the psychosocial needs of the patient and the family.
  • Keep the room's energy and mood conducive to healing: patience, encouragement, calm energy.
