Dysrhythmias

Reading a Rhythm Strip

WaveWhat to Know

PR intervalFrom the start of the P to the start of the QRS: the pause while the impulse goes through the AV node. 0.12 to 0.20 seconds. Longer: something is wrong with conduction between the atria and the ventricles.

R to R intervalThe tip of one R to the tip of the next. Equal intervals that "march out" make a regular rhythm.

Measuring a Six-Second Strip

  • One small box: 0.04 seconds. Five small boxes, one big box: 0.20 seconds.
  • Six seconds: every strip on a test. The marks at the top are 3 seconds apart.
  • Heart rate: the QRSs in the six seconds, times 10.
  • Regular or not: march out the R to R intervals.

Two Ways to Sort Dysrhythmias

By Rate: Early, Slow, Fast

  • Premature complexes: early beats.
  • Bradydysrhythmias: a heart rate below 60.
  • Tachydysrhythmias: a heart rate above 100.
  • Why 60 and 100: the SA node fires 60 to 100 times a minute on its own.

By Site of Origin

  • Sinus: from the SA node.
  • Atrial: from the atria.
  • Ventricular: from the ventricles.

Rhythms and Cardiac Output

For further study, see Med Surg II Week 2 – Dysrhythmias.

Cardiac output, and what low cardiac output means, is taught in the Cardiovascular review

Bradydysrhythmias: Below 60

  • Myocardial oxygen demand: reduced, a good thing. Beta blockers slow the heart rate on purpose for it.
  • Coronary perfusion: the coronary arteries perfuse in diastole. A slow rate prolongs diastole: more perfusion time. Too slow: perfusion falls, and angina may start.
  • Cardiac output: may be tolerated if the BP is adequate. An athlete resting at 54 with a good BP goes about the day. Another patient at 54 is symptomatic.

Tachydysrhythmias: Above 100

  • Myocardial oxygen demand: raised by the extra workload, a bad thing. Worse in a compromised heart.
  • Coronary perfusion: a shorter diastole shortens perfusion time, and angina may start.
  • Cardiac output: may rise at first, with the BP: compensation. Faster or sustained, the ventricles cannot fill: stroke volume falls, and with it cardiac output and BP. 104 is usually tolerable. 120 or 130 cannot be sustained.

Either, if not tolerated, can lead to: myocardial ischemia or infarct, dysrhythmias, hypotension, heart failure.

Blood pressure is the indirect measure of cardiac output. A rate outside 60 to 100: go check on the patient, and take a set of vital signs.

Key Features of a Sustained Tachy- or Bradydysrhythmia

What Too Slow or Too Fast Looks Like

  • Heart: angina, from a lack of coronary perfusion. An S3 or S4, a gallop. Palpitations, with a tachycardia. Hypotension.
  • Brain, poorly perfused: restlessness, anxiety, confusion. Dizziness, syncope.
  • Lungs: shortness of air, tachypnea. Pulmonary crackles, a sign of left-sided heart failure. Orthopnea.
  • Periphery: a pulse deficit. JVD, a sign of right-sided heart failure. Pale, cool skin, diaphoresis, delayed cap refill. Weakness, fatigue.
  • Kidneys, poorly perfused: decreased urine output. Watch it closely.
  • GI: nausea and vomiting.

The Atria Versus the Ventricles

Atrial Dysrhythmias: the Ventricular Rate Matters

  • The atrial kick is lost in AFib, and inconsistent in flutter.
  • The ventricular rate is what matters: the left ventricle is the powerhouse chamber. The AV node, the gatekeeper, lets only some atrial impulses through.
  • Controlled: AFib with a ventricular rate of 90 is not ideal, but livable.
  • Too fast: no time for the ventricles to fill. Stroke volume and cardiac output fall.

PVCs

Typically do not perfuse. With bigeminy, only every other beat perfuses.

VTach

Seriously decreases cardiac output: too fast for the ventricles to fill. A pulse means it is perfusing.

VFib

No cardiac output: the ventricles only quiver. A deadly rhythm.

← Least severeMost severe →

Untreated, each slips into the next, and the further it goes, the less well treatment works.

You can live with AFib. You cannot live with VFib.

Sinus Rhythms

The Two Normal Sinus Rhythms

  • Normal sinus rhythm (NSR): the SA node fires 60 to 100, with normal conduction. A P before every QRS, all alike, and the R to R regular. Often just called "sinus".
  • Sinus arrhythmia: the same, but the R to R is not quite regular. Changes in intrathoracic pressure with breathing change the SA node's firing. A normal physiological phenomenon, seen in young, healthy people. Not treated: an assessment finding.
RhythmOn the StripCausesWhat the Patient Looks LikeTreatment
Sinus bradycardia
  • The SA node fires below 60.
  • A P before every QRS.
  • Vagal stimulation: carotid sinus massage (provider only), vomiting or gagging, Valsalva maneuvers, eyeball pressure, parasympathomimetic drugs.
  • Digoxin toxicity.
  • Hyperkalemia: slows depolarization.
  • MI.
  • Normal in a well-conditioned athlete, and during sleep.
  • May be asymptomatic, but for the slow rate.
  • Symptomatic: lightheaded, nauseous, about to pass out, a low BP. The key features above.
  • Not symptomatic: monitor, and do not treat.
  • Symptomatic: IV atropine.
  • Still symptomatic: transcutaneous pacing, right away.
  • Persistent: a permanent pacemaker.
Sinus tachycardia
  • The SA node fires above 100, from vagal inhibition.
  • A P before every QRS.
  • Physical activity, anxiety, pain, stress, fever.
  • Anemia: the heart speeds up to circulate the red cells there are.
  • Hypoxia.
  • Compensating for a low BP: dehydration or hypovolemia, MI, heart failure. A low stroke volume raises the heart rate.
  • May be asymptomatic, but for the fast rate.
  • Not tolerated: palpitations, with the key features above.
  • Treat the cause: fluid replacement, an analgesic, an antipyretic, an anxiolytic such as a benzodiazepine.
  • Beta blockers reduce the rate and myocardial oxygen demand. The cause comes first.

Atrial Dysrhythmias

RhythmOn the StripCausesWhat the Patient Looks LikeTreatment
Premature atrial
contraction (PAC)
  • An ectopic focus in the atria fires before the SA node: one early beat.
  • Equal R to R intervals, one early beat, then equal again.
  • Usually benign.
  • Electrolytes: check them, especially magnesium, calcium and potassium.
  • Stress, stimulants such as caffeine, atrial pathology.
  • Benign most of the time. In a hospital patient, it may be pathologic.
  • New, or more frequent: may be about to convert to AFib. Time can be of the essence.
  • Benign: nothing.
  • New or more frequent: check the electrolytes and tell the provider. The drugs given for AFib may prevent it.
Atrial fibrillation
(AFib)
  • Many ectopic foci fire at once, and the atria quiver. No P waves: tiny, irregular "f" waves.
  • Atrial rate above 400, ventricular rate 100 to 175. R to R irregularly irregular.
  • Underlying heart disease, almost any.
  • Electrolyte imbalance, hypoxia.
  • Cardiac surgery: often postoperatively.
  • The most common dysrhythmia, more common with age.
  • Depends on the ventricular rate, how long the rhythm has lasted, and the cardiovascular status.
  • Onset usually fast: the key features of a tachydysrhythmia.
  • Clots: blood pools in the quivering atria. An embolus from the left atrium causes a stroke.
  • Rate control first, then rhythm control, and preventing an embolic stroke. See below.
Atrial flutter
  • One ectopic focus: recurring, regular sawtooth "F" waves, not P waves.
  • Atrial rate may be above 250, the ventricular rate slower. R to R usually regular, sometimes irregular.
  • Underlying heart condition, any. Rare in a healthy heart, and less common than AFib.
  • Electrolyte imbalance: check them.
  • As with AFib: depends on the ventricular rate, how long, and the cardiovascular status. Onset usually fast.
  • At risk for emboli too.
  • As for AFib. See below.
Paroxysmal
supraventricular
tachycardia (PSVT)
  • A run of repeated premature beats, from a focus anywhere above the bundle of His.
  • Usually starts with a PAC, and stops as abruptly as it began.
  • In a normal heart: overexertion, emotional stress, stimulants. Not terribly uncommon.
  • More serious: digoxin toxicity, heart disease.
  • Depends on how long it lasts and how fast the ventricular rate.
  • Too fast: the key features of a tachydysrhythmia.
  • Sustained SVT does not stop on its own: it needs treating.
  • On the monitor: vagal maneuvers, then adenosine, then cardioversion. See below.

Ventricular Dysrhythmias

RhythmOn the StripCausesWhat the Patient Looks LikeTreatment
Premature ventricular
contraction (PVC)
  • From an ectopic focus in the ventricles: early, with no normal P wave. Wide and distorted beside the normal QRS.
  • Patterns: bigeminy, trigeminy, quadrigeminy. Unifocal or multifocal: multifocal is worse.
  • Stimulants, electrolytes, hypoxia, fever, exercise, emotional stress, cardiovascular disease.
  • Isolated: can be benign. Chronic for some patients.
  • New, or more frequent: may be about to convert to VTach, a crisis. Watch closely, and tell the provider.
  • Treat the cause.
  • Drugs: beta blockers, lidocaine or amiodarone.
Ventricular
tachycardia (VTach)
  • 3 or more PVCs in a row: an ectopic focus in the ventricles takes control, and the atria do not contract.
  • Rate 150 to 200, usually regular. No P wave, no measurable PR.
  • MI, CAD, significant electrolyte abnormalities, heart failure, drug toxicity.
  • Symptomatic very quickly, unless it converts back.
  • Pulse or pulseless: the first question. No pulse, no perfusion.
  • ACLS, by the pulse. Pulseless: defibrillation.
  • An antidysrhythmic: a beta blocker, calcium channel blocker or amiodarone.
  • Electrolytes: checked, and replaced at once.
Ventricular
fibrillation (VFib)
  • Irregular waveforms of varying shapes and sizes.
  • No QRSs.
  • Untreated VTach slips into VFib.
  • The ventricles just quiver: no effective contractions. Cardiac arrest, unconscious.
  • CPR, ACLS, defibrillation. Call a code.

Treatment

For further study, see Med Surg II Week 2 – Atrial Dysrhythmias and Med Surg II Week 2 – Ventricular Dysrhythmias.

Always check the electrolytes in a patient with a dysrhythmia. Imbalances cause PACs, AFib, flutter, PVCs and VTach. In VTach, replace them at once.

The electrolytes that matter, potassium, calcium and magnesium, are taught in the Cardiovascular review

AFib and Atrial Flutter

Three Goals of Treatment

  1. Rate control, the ventricular rate: the priority. Beta blockers such as metoprolol, or calcium channel blockers such as diltiazem and verapamil. IV at first.
  2. Rhythm control: amiodarone or dofetilide. IV at first.
  3. Preventing an embolic stroke: warfarin, or another anticoagulant.

Stable, but Symptomatic

  • Slow the ventricular rate: an IV calcium channel blocker, beta blocker, digoxin or amiodarone.
  • Often a bolus and a drip: the bolus raises the drug level at once, and the drip keeps it there. A drip alone takes too long.

Unstable

  • Hemodynamically compromised: synchronized cardioversion. See below.
  • Emergent cardioversion: low-molecular-weight heparin or a heparin drip is started as well.

Before Cardioverting AFib

  • AFib for more than 48 hours: clots may have formed in the atria. Cardioversion restores the kick and throws the clot to the brain: a stroke.
  • Anticoagulation, usually warfarin: 3 to 6 weeks before cardioversion, and 3 to 4 weeks after.
  • A TEE may be done first, to make sure there are no clots in the atria.

Long-term anticoagulation when treatment fails, warfarin and the newer drugs, is taught in the Hematology review

Drugs often work, but fail often and can be toxic. Two treatments are not drugs:

Catheter Ablation

  • Invasive: destroys the irritable focus causing the dysrhythmia.
  • First: electrophysiology (EP) studies and mapping, to locate the focus.

Maze Procedure

  • Surgical: incisions in the atrium disrupt the re-entrant circuits.
  • Leaves one path from the SA node to the AV node, like a children's maze.

PSVT

Vagal Maneuvers

First, on the monitor.

Adenosine

If they fail: IV push.

Synchronized Cardioversion

If both fail, or the patient becomes hemodynamically unstable.

Vagal Maneuvers for PSVT

  • Valsalva, the most effective: bear down as for a bowel movement. Hold the breath 10 to 15 seconds, hard enough to distend the neck veins, then breathe.
  • Coughing: a ventilated patient can be suctioned to make them cough.
  • Carotid sinus massage: provider only. Never the nurse.
  • Diving reflex: the face in cold water. Rarely used now.

Cardioversion Versus Defibrillation

Synchronized Cardioversion

  • For: a hemodynamically unstable supraventricular tachydysrhythmia: AFib, flutter, SVT. Needs an R wave.
  • Sync on: the shock lands on the R wave of the QRS. Unsynchronized, a shock on the wrong part of the complex can trigger a life-threatening dysrhythmia.
  • Energy: 50 to 100 joules at first, raised if needed: 50 to 200 in all.
  • The patient: usually elective, awake, and sedated first unless it is an emergency. A consent form, and the EKG monitor.
  • Pulseless after: the QRS is lost. Sync off, and defibrillate.
  • After: a patent airway and oxygen, vital signs and level of consciousness, watch for dysrhythmias, emotional support. Document whether it worked.

Defibrillation

  • For: VFib and pulseless VTach: no cardiac output. An emergency: call a code.
  • Sync off: there is no QRS to sense, so with the sync on it would never fire. It fires the moment the button is pushed.
  • Energy: more than cardioversion. Begins at 200 joules, up to 360.
  • The patient: unconscious, on the cardiac monitor.
All clear before any shock: no one touching the patient or the bed.
If it's VFib, you defib, and call a code.

The Drugs

For further study, see Med Surg II Week 2 – Sinus Dysrhythmias and Med Surg II Week 2 – Atrial Dysrhythmias.

DrugWhat It DoesUsed ForHow It Is Given
Atropine
  • Vagolytic: blocks the vagus nerve, the main nerve of the parasympathetic nervous system.
  • Speeds up the heart rate.
  • An anticholinergic: with the vagus nerve's brake off, the SA node fires faster and impulses cross the AV node faster.
  • Symptomatic sinus bradycardia.
  • 1 mg IV every 3 to 5 minutes, 3 mg max. Aim: a rate back in 60 to 100.
  • Not working: transcutaneous pacing.
Adenosine
  • Slows the heart rate.
  • Briefly blocks conduction through the AV node, which most PSVT needs to keep going: the rhythm breaks, and the SA node takes back over.
  • PSVT, when vagal maneuvers fail.
  • IV push, very fast, then a rapid NS flush: a very short half-life. Often two syringes of saline, pushed together.
  • Onset: 10 to 40 seconds. Lasts 1 to 2 minutes.
  • A pause on the strip, a period of asystole. The patient may be aware of it: tell them first.
Beta blockers,
such as metoprolol
  • Slow the heart rate, reducing myocardial oxygen demand.
  • Slow conduction through the AV node: fewer of the atria's impulses reach the ventricles.
  • Labetalol also blocks alpha receptors, so the arteries widen too: mostly a blood pressure drug.
  • AFib and flutter: rate control.
  • Sinus tachycardia, once the cause is treated.
  • PVCs, and VTach.
  • IV at first for AFib and flutter, often a bolus and a drip.
Amiodarone
  • An antidysrhythmic: rhythm control.
  • Prolongs repolarization: the heart's cells take longer before they can fire again, which suppresses fast rhythms from the atria and the ventricles.
  • AFib and flutter: rhythm control. Also slows the ventricular rate when the patient is stable but symptomatic.
  • PVCs, and VTach.
  • IV at first for AFib and flutter, often a bolus and a drip.
Adenosine and atropine both start with A. Adenosine slows the heart rate. Atropine speeds it up. Do not mix them up.

Beta blockers, calcium channel blockers and digoxin, their examples and what to watch for, are taught in the Heart Disease review

Pacing

For further study, see Med Surg II Week 2 – Sinus Dysrhythmias.

Transcutaneous Pacing

  • When: symptomatic sinus bradycardia that atropine does not fix. Right here, right now.
  • How: pads on the patient, and the device paces the heart through the skin. It stands in for the SA node.

Permanent Pacemaker

  • When: a persistent bradycardia that responds to nothing else. The provider decides.
  • How: under the skin, usually just below the clavicle. Fires when the SA node is not cooperating.
  • Paces as needed: every sixth beat in one patient, every beat in another.

ICDs and Device Restrictions

When an ICD Fires

  • The patient feels the shock: a sudden, hard thump or kick in the chest.
  • One shock, and the patient feels well after: call the provider that day.
  • More than one shock, or chest pain, dizziness or shortness of breath: call 911.

Living With a Pacemaker or ICD

  • For the first few weeks: keep the arm on the device side below the shoulder, and lift nothing heavy with it, while the leads settle in.
  • Magnets and cell phones at least 6 inches from the device: no phone in a shirt pocket over it.
  • Carry the device ID card at all times.
