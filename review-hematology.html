Hemoglobin and Hematocrit

Note trends, more so than stand-alone values. An H&H of 9.2 and 28.1 two days after surgery, up from 8.1 and 24.6 the day before, is trending upward: keep an eye on it.

Low Hematocrit

  • True low: anemia. Anemia is a low hematocrit, with a cause behind it.
  • Causes: bleeding, bone marrow failure, dietary deficiency, renal disease.
  • False low: fluid retention, as in heart failure or kidney failure, adds plasma. The percentage drops without anemia.

High Hematocrit

  • True high: polycythemia.
  • False high: dehydration leaves little plasma. The percentage rises without polycythemia.
The bus analogy: RBCs and oxygen.
  • RBC: the bus. Without the buses, oxygen cannot get where it belongs.
  • Hgb: the seats.
  • Iron: what the seats are made of.
  • Oxygen: the passengers.
  • O2 sat: how full the buses are, about 95% or higher.
  • Hct: how much of the highway, the blood, is made up of buses.

The WBC Differential

The five WBCs: Never Let Monkeys Eat Bananas.
  • Never: neutrophils, 40 to 70%.
  • Let: lymphocytes, 20 to 25%.
  • Monkeys: monocytes, 3 to 8%.
  • Eat: eosinophils, 2 to 4%.
  • Bananas: basophils, under 1%.

When the Neutrophils Rise

  • The most plentiful WBC, and the first to arrive at an acute bacterial infection.
  • Their percentage goes up and the others go down: the differential always adds up to 100%.
  • The mature neutrophils (segs) respond, and the baby neutrophils (bands) are released too: a shift to the left, a sign of the onset of an acute bacterial infection.

Heparin Drips

Heparin Drip: Labs and Adverse Reactions

  • Labs: the aPTT or the anti-Xa, by protocol, about 6 hours after the drip starts and after each rate change. The rate is titrated to the result. The platelets too, for HIT.
  • Bleeding: the main adverse reaction. Gums, urine, stool, wounds, new bruising, a falling H&H. Report it at once: the drip may need to stop. Antidote: protamine sulfate.
  • HIT (heparin-induced thrombocytopenia): the platelets fall by half or more, usually 5 to 10 days in, and new clots can form.

HIT, its lab and stopping the heparin, are taught in Hematologic Issues in Critical Care

Oral Anticoagulants

For further study, see Med Surg II Week 1 – Valvular Problems and Med Surg II Week 2 – Atrial Dysrhythmias.

Warfarin

  • Used for: a mechanical valve, for life. AFib, long term, when treatment fails.
  • Labs: the INR, monitored regularly. Normal 0.8 to 1.1. Therapeutic with a prosthetic valve: 2.5 to 3.5. Thin, but not too thin: any higher risks bleeding.
  • Antidote: vitamin K, for any signs of bleeding. FFP can also be given.
  • Diet: vitamin K reverses warfarin. Foods loaded with it, such as kale greens, are eaten consistently, never binged.
  • Bleeding: an electric razor, not a straight edge. Report any bleeding or excessive bleeding. See below for bleeding precautions.

The Newer Oral Anticoagulants

  • Used for: non-valvular AFib, as alternatives to warfarin.
  • Labs: no routine lab testing, the advantage. Creatinine checked before giving: contraindicated with impaired renal function.
  • Dosing: dabigatran and apixaban twice daily. Rivaroxaban and edoxaban once daily.
  • Drawbacks: cost, and more than once-daily dosing.

Bleeding Precautions

For further study, see Med Surg I Week 7 – Cirrhosis and Med Surg I Week 7 – Cancer.

For a patient at risk of bleeding: a high prothrombin time, as in cirrhosis, where the liver lacks prothrombin, or a really low platelet count (thrombocytopenia).

What Bleeding Precautions Include

  • Monitor the platelets, PT and PTT. With thrombocytopenia, serious bleeding may not be apparent until the platelet count is under 50,000.
  • Assess the oral cavity: the gums are one of the first places bleeding shows.
  • Monitor for ecchymosis, purpura and petechiae.
  • Protect from falls: a fall with a prolonged bleeding time risks a head injury and a subarachnoid hemorrhage.
  • No aspirin, an antiplatelet that only increases the bleeding. No alcohol, spicy foods or bulky foods.
  • No injections: the patient may bleed considerably. Pressure on any bleeding for 5 minutes, and considerably longer than usual after an IM injection.
  • No vigorous nose blowing or straining with BMs: bearing down raises intrathoracic pressure, which can rupture esophageal varices. Stool softeners.
  • Soft toothbrush. No rectal temperatures or enemas.
  • Teach the patient all of these, especially when going home on them.

Thrombocytopenic precautions in critical care are taught in Hematologic Issues in Critical Care
