Advanced Respiratory Assessment

FactorWhat to Assess
Neurological status
  • Altered level of consciousness or sedation: can the patient maintain a patent airway?
  • Effective cough and a gag reflex?
  • Aware enough to follow instructions to deep breathe and cough?
Pain, procedures, conditions
  • Where the pain is matters. Chest surgery (bypass) and big abdominal surgery make deep breaths hard: watch for, treat and prevent atelectasis and pneumonia.
  • Anxiety and panic: breathing 40 times a minute blows off CO2 and prevents the deep breaths needed for effective gas exchange.
Weakness and coughing
  • Weakness follows an ICU stay, big surgery, big trauma or a long time in bed, even with mobilization.
  • A weak patient cannot protect the airway as effectively. Cough is a major indicator of respiratory function and risk.
  • Is the cough effective? How frequent? What does the sputum look like?
  • Ineffective cough: pre-medicate for pain or change position.
  • Oral or nasal suction may be needed.
  • Concerning: coughing that is not productive and is blocking the airway.
Medications
  • Some help respiratory function and some inhibit it.
  • Watch pain medication and sedation: heavy opioid use, a sleepy patient, recent anesthesia.

Acute Respiratory Failure

Inability of the respiratory system to provide oxygen and/or remove carbon dioxide from the body. Most common admitting diagnosis in critical care units, as the primary problem (severe pneumonia) or the result of another condition (major surgery with blood loss, time on a ventilator, sepsis).

Oxygenation Failure: Hypoxemia

  • PaO2 less than 60 (partial pressure of arterial oxygen), found on an ABG.
  • PaO2 is the most accurate oxygen level. An O2 sat is affected by perfusion to the fingers, warmth and arterial disease.
  • See below for causes.

Ventilatory Failure: Hypercapnia

  • PaCO2 greater than 50 on an ABG. A venous CO2 is not as diagnostic as an arterial one.
  • Can be related to chronic respiratory failure, but not mutually exclusive. A stressor such as a respiratory infection overwhelms a system already compromised by COPD.
  • Also related to hypoventilation and/or V/Q mismatch.
  • Hypercapnia significantly increases cerebral blood flow, so CNS changes occur: anxiety, restlessness, rising intracranial pressure. LOC changes come more quickly than in oxygenation failure.
  • Hypoventilation leaves CO2 sitting in the alveoli: respiratory acidosis develops quickly. The kidneys cannot compensate the way the lungs can.

A patient is not usually labelled as one type. Look at the clinical picture and decide whether it is both, or primarily an oxygen problem.

Five Mechanisms of Oxygenation Failure

MechanismWhat Happens
Hypoventilation
  • Alveoli do not get the oxygen they need from outside. Rate or depth of respirations severely decreased (slow and deep at 4 to 6 breaths a minute, or a normal rate of 16 but very shallow).
  • CO2 problems often come with it.
  • Examples: CNS depression (overdose, brain injury), after anesthesia, pain.
Intrapulmonary shunting
  • Blood flow reaches the alveoli, but the alveoli cannot exchange gas.
  • Examples: alveoli that are not compliant (as in emphysema) or are filled with something (as in chronic bronchitis or pneumonia).
V/Q mismatch
  • Ventilation-perfusion mismatching. Gas exchange depends on the balance between the ventilated (V) and the perfused (Q) areas of the lungs.
  • Example: ventilation without perfusion (as in pulmonary embolism).
Diffusion defects
  • Defect in the alveolar-capillary membrane, where the alveoli meet the pulmonary capillaries.
  • Examples: fluid accumulation (as in pneumonia) or fibrotic changes (as in COPD or pulmonary fibrosis).
Decreased barometric pressure
  • Example: high altitude.

Oxygenation also fails with low cardiac output (the heart is not pumping oxygenated blood where it needs to go) and low hemoglobin (too anemic to carry oxygen to the tissues).

How Respiratory Failure Is Recognized

Signs That Point to Respiratory Failure

  • Increased work of breathing.
  • Increasing O2 needs. Turned up from 2 L to 6 L with no improvement: not good.
  • Chest x-ray.
  • Labs: H&H and ABG (PaO2, PaCO2).

Interventions for Respiratory Failure

InterventionWhat to Do
Maintain a patent airway
  • Head tilt, chin lift. Get the patient into the best position first: sitting up, airway open.
  • Oral airway, bag mask, or intubation if needed.
Optimize O2 delivery
  • Goal: PaO2 above 60, SpO2 above 90%.
  • Start with supplemental O2, turn it up, then advance to higher levels.
Minimize O2 demand
  • Avoid physical activity: no bed bath, no turning, no walking.
  • Even a breathing treatment may have to wait. Involve respiratory therapy.
  • May ultimately need to sedate and paralyze.
Treat the cause
  • Provide rest, reduce fever, treat pain.
Declining quickly: give the oxygen the patient needs, even in chronic COPD. CO2 and oxygen toxicity are dealt with as they come.

Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS)

Most severe version of acute respiratory failure, and a disease process of its own. Anyone in ARDS is in acute respiratory failure.

CriterionWhat It Means
Acute onset
  • Within 1 week of a clinical insult.
  • Abrupt. Not someone who became very sick from a chronic respiratory issue.
Bilateral pulmonary opacities
  • Both lungs "whited out" on chest x-ray. Dark lung means airflow and gas exchange.
  • Not explained by another condition such as pneumonia or pulmonary fibrosis.
Altered PaO2/FiO2 ratio
  • Arterial oxygen (PaO2) not responding to the oxygen being given (FiO2).
  • Measured while on positive airway pressure: PEEP or CPAP.
  • The number determines diagnosis and severity.

Causes of ARDS

Direct Causes

  • Aspiration of gastric contents
  • Fat embolism
  • Inhalation of toxic gases
  • Multisystem trauma (chest and/or lung injury)
  • Near-drowning
  • Pneumonia

Indirect Causes

  • Burns
  • Cardiopulmonary bypass
  • Drug overdose
  • Fractures, especially of the pelvis or long bones (through fat embolism)
  • Multiple transfusions
  • Multisystem trauma (without chest and/or lung injury)
  • Pancreatitis
  • Sepsis
Highest-frequency risk factors: new-onset pneumonia, sepsis, aspiration and trauma. Two or more together, such as pneumonia followed by sepsis, is very high risk.

Pathophysiology of ARDS

From Lung Injury to Hypoxemia

  1. Acute and diffuse injury to the lungs.
  2. Inflammatory response: a cell-mediated, overly aggressive immune response damages the alveolar-capillary membrane.
  3. Massive platelet aggregation and intravascular thrombi. Sputum is often blood-tinged or pink.
  4. The membrane becomes very permeable. Fluid, protein and blood cells leak into the alveoli, and massive fluid leakage throughout the body causes edema.
  5. Alveolar flooding: noncardiogenic pulmonary edema (not heart failure), shunting, V/Q mismatch, decreased compliance, hypoxemia. The edema sitting in the lungs puts the patient at very high risk of pneumonia.
  6. Pulmonary hypertension causes further shunting. Dead space grows and surfactant is not produced.

Assessment Findings in ARDS

Acute Onset, Not Yet Intubated

  • Dyspnea, tachypnea.
  • Hypoxemia not responding to supplemental O2 (refractory hypoxemia). FiO2 goes up through high flow, CPAP and intubation, and PaO2 and SpO2 still do not rise.
  • Fine crackles from fluid in the alveoli. Often no breath sounds at all.
  • Restlessness, disorientation, decreasing LOC.
  • Pulse and temperature may increase.
  • PaO2 continues to decrease and dyspnea becomes severe. Central cyanosis when severe enough.

Intubated Patient

  • Usually sedated, paralyzed and ventilated.
  • Peak pressures increasing: how the ventilator shows decreased compliance.
  • Increasing O2 needs, with no rise in SpO2 or PaO2.

ARDS Interventions

Primary goal: adequate oxygenation.
InterventionWhat It Does
Mechanical ventilation
  • Usually needed. Not many patients manage on CPAP or BiPAP.
  • Settings are protective: as minimal as possible. Max settings cause permanent lung damage.
  • The PaO2 goal may be 50 to 55 instead of above 60.
ECMO
  • Bypasses the lungs and oxygenates for them, giving the lungs a chance to rest.
Corticosteroids
  • Dampen the inflammatory response that is causing ARDS.
Prone positioning
  • Patient is turned onto the stomach.
  • Brings in alveoli that are not used as often and are less likely to be damaged.
Therapeutic hypothermia
  • Turns the body's metabolic demands down very low for a period of time.
  • Preserves function, especially in the brain, when PaO2 is very low.

ARDS Consequences

Problems Common After ARDS

  • PTSD, anxiety and depression: more than half of survivors, within two years of recovery.
  • Weakness, mobility issues, breathing issues.
  • Nausea, swelling, memory issues, fatigue.

Multidisciplinary Care

  • PT/OT: mobility, to prevent weakness.
  • RT and MD: ventilator management and sedation needs.
  • Nutrition: usually a Dobhoff tube and high levels of supplemental nutrition.
  • Psychosocial and mental health: early treatment and awareness, beginning in the ICU and continuing through discharge and beyond.
  • Outpatient critical care follow-up for long-term needs.

Pulmonary Embolism

What Can Lodge in the Pulmonary Circulation

  • Clot from a DVT: most common, from a lower extremity.
  • Fat emboli.
  • Septic vegetation, from a heart valve in sepsis.
  • Catheter fragment, from a PICC or central line.
  • Amniotic fluid emboli in pregnant women.

Most PEs and DVTs are never identified, because the lungs have an enormous capacity to compensate. One that does present is usually very dangerous.

Massive PE: systolic hypotension below 90 related to the PE. Mortality rate of 30 to 80%.

Shunting, V/Q Mismatch and the Death Spiral

Shunting and V/Q Mismatch in a PE

  • Airflow is there. The patient can take breaths in.
  • Blood cannot get to the alveoli to perform gas exchange.
  • Blood flow beyond the occlusion stops, so there is no gas exchange beyond it.

Pulmonary Embolism

Blood cannot flow freely through the pulmonary vasculature. Pulmonary pressures rise.

Increased Pulmonary Vascular Resistance

The lungs back up against the blockage.

RV Dilation

Increased RV wall stress and ineffective contraction. The septum bows into the LV and decreases LV filling.

Hypotension

RV hypoperfusion and ischemia, and more fluid given by the physician, dilate the RV further. Can end in right heart failure and death.

Two worries in a PE: hypotension, and no ability to perform gas exchange because a huge embolism is blocking blood flow.

PE Identification and Assessment Findings

Assessment Findings

  • Symptoms are usually vague and nondescript, with no hallmark sign.
  • Classic: pleuritic chest pain, shortness of breath, hypoxia.
  • Less common: tachypnea, crackles, accentuated S2 heart sound, tachycardia, fever.
  • Suspect a PE in any patient with respiratory distress that cannot be easily explained.
  • Pregnancy is a very high risk for PE.

Who is at high risk for a PE is taught in the Respiratory Diseases review

TestWhat It Shows
Chest x-ray
  • Does not show a PE. It shows ventilation, and ventilation is not the problem.
  • Lungs look clear while the patient cannot breathe.
D-dimer
  • Lab: a product of fibrin (clot) breakdown.
  • Positive can indicate thrombus formation.
  • Quick and easy. Points toward a PE but is not diagnostic alone.
V/Q scan
  • Noninvasive nuclear medicine scan of airflow and blood flow in the lungs. Can show dead space.
  • Once the hallmark test. Little used now that CT is so available.
Spiral CT scan
  • Preferred: the most accurate.
  • Pregnant patients cannot have a CT scan.
Pulmonary angiography
  • Direct anatomical visualization.
  • Invasive and difficult. Done in the cath lab, and not often.

PE Treatment

Less Severe

At home on blood thinners: enoxaparin, or Xa inhibitors such as apixaban long term. Follow-up, and risk factors addressed.

Severe

IV heparin and a bolus immediately, then transition to enoxaparin. Warfarin started immediately as well, for long-term prevention and management.

Massive

Surgical or catheter embolectomy. EKOS catheter: ultrasound with heparin, placed right in the pulmonary circulation. ECMO when very severe.

IVC Filter, Oxygenation and Prevention

  • IVC filter: often placed. Sits in the inferior vena cava to catch clots that dislodge from the lower extremities.
  • Optimize oxygenation acutely, as well as you can. Intubation may be needed but does not solve the problem, which is blood flow to the lungs.
  • Prevention: heparin shots or enoxaparin, SCDs, mobilizing.

Other Respiratory Topics and Diagnostics

Hemodynamic Monitoring in Respiratory Problems

MonitoringWhat It Tells You
Capnography
  • Monitors CO2 as end-tidal CO2, shown as a waveform.
  • Read through a nasal cannula that measures CO2, or through the ventilator.
  • Used to measure effective CPR.
Arterial line
  • Usually in the radial artery.
  • Gives frequent ABG access: PaO2, PaCO2 and pH as often as every hour or every 30 minutes.
PA pressure
  • Pulmonary artery pressure. Looks at pulmonary vascular resistance.
SvO2
  • Venous oxygen saturation, also called mixed venous: the oxygen content of blood returning to the right side of the heart after perfusing the body.
  • Normal: 60 to 80%. The tissues take only about 20 to 40%.
  • Low (30 to 50%): the body is extracting more. Not enough oxygen for a very high demand.
