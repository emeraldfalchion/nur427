Transfusions and Blood Products

The Blood Products

ProductWhat It IsGiven ForGiving It
Packed red blood cells (PRBCs)
  • RBCs only: no plasma, WBCs or platelets.
  • About 250 to 350 mL.
  • Severe anemia.
  • ABO and Rh compatibility, by a type and crossmatch.
  • See below for giving PRBCs.
Platelets
  • Prepared from fresh whole blood.
  • About 30 to 60 mL.
  • Bleeding caused by thrombocytopenia. See below for the count.
  • Examples: chemotherapy.
  • ABO compatibility not a factor.
  • Agitate the bag periodically: platelets clump.
  • As fast as tolerated.
Fresh frozen plasma (FFP)
  • The liquid portion of whole blood, frozen. About 250 mL.
  • Rich in clotting factors, such as prothrombin. No platelets.
  • Bleeding from a lack of clotting factors.
  • Rarely, a fluid volume expander.
  • Examples: liver issues, hemophilia, von Willebrand's.
  • ABO compatibility required.
  • Used within 24 hours of thawing.
Albumin
  • A colloid, prepared from plasma: a plasma volume expander.
  • Pulls fluid into the vascular space and keeps it there.
  • 5%, equal to plasma, or 25%, hypertonic.
  • Low albumin: fluid shifts out to the third space, and the patient becomes very edematous.
  • 25% for a patient in a really bad way.
  • Examples: a liver patient.
  • Compatibility not a factor.

When to give platelets, by the count and active bleeding, is taught in Hematologic Issues in Critical Care

Platelets are not clotting factors. Both enhance clotting, but not in the same way. Thrombocytopenia: give platelets. A lack of clotting factors: give FFP, the plasma.

What Makes Blood Compatible

  • Two sets of antigens: ABO (A, B, both or neither) and Rh (present or absent).
  • A recipient can receive only antigens they already have. One they lack causes a hemolytic reaction.
  • O negative: universal donor. No A, B or Rh antigen, so it can go to anyone.
  • AB positive: universal recipient. Every antigen already, so it can receive any blood.

When PRBCs Are Given

Know the H&H before hanging PRBCs. The guidelines, for a hemodynamically stable patient without active bleeding:

HemoglobinTransfusion
Under 6 g/dLRecommended, except in exceptional circumstances.
6 to 7 g/dLGenerally likely. Is the patient symptomatic: BP low, HR high from compensating?
7 to 8 g/dLMay be appropriate, after evaluating the clinical status: orthopedic or cardiac surgery, stable cardiovascular disease.
8 to 10 g/dLGenerally not. Considered for some: symptomatic anemia, ongoing bleeding, acute coronary syndrome with ischemia, hematology and oncology patients with severe thrombocytopenia at risk of bleeding.
Over 10 g/dLGenerally not, except in exceptional circumstances.

Giving PRBCs

Before sending for the blood

Type and crossmatch within the last 72 hours. Premedicate as ordered: acetaminophen for a fever risk, diphenhydramine for an itching risk.

IV 20 gauge or larger, 18 ideal: a smaller gauge can break down the RBCs. Y tubing with a filter, primed with NS. NS only, never dextrose or LR: they can hemolyze the cells, and LR's calcium can cause clots.

Within 30 minutes of the blood reaching the floor

Start it, or take it back to the blood bank. Never left on the floor or in the medication refrigerator. The final identity check is done at the bedside, by two RNs.

First 15 minutes

Teach the patient to report anything unusual at once: chills, SOB, itching, back pain. Cold at the IV site is expected. Baseline vital signs, by the RN.

Start slowly: 1 to 2 mL/min (60 to 120 mL/hr). The RN stays with the patient for the first 15 to 30 minutes, per protocol.

At 15 minutes

Vital signs. No reaction: increase the rate, to infuse in about 2 hours. With a risk for fluid volume excess, such as an older patient with heart failure or kidney failure, stretch it toward the 4 hours.

Every hour, and at the end

Vital signs.

4 hours at most

The longest blood can ever hang: after that, bacterial proliferation. Not in by then: take it down, document, and let the provider know. Never rush the rest in.

After

NS through the tubing, for every last drop. Know the orders: furosemide between units with a risk for fluid volume excess, another unit, a repeat H&H.

Transfusion Reactions

When a Reaction Is Suspected

  1. Stop the transfusion, always first, to assess what is going on.
  2. New tubing primed with NS, at the same IV site. Not the saline on the blood tubing, which is still full of blood.
  3. Assess the patient. Notify the provider and the blood bank.
  4. Recheck the ID tags and numbers.
  5. Monitor vital signs and urine output: a hemolytic reaction can shut the kidneys down.
  6. Treat the symptoms, per the provider's orders.
  7. Save the blood bag and tubing for the blood bank, which rechecks everything.
  8. Complete the transfusion reaction report form. Collect specimens to evaluate for hemolysis.
ReactionCauseSigns and SymptomsNursing Response
Acute hemolytic (AHTR)Incompatible blood: the wrong blood. The most life-threatening.
  • Immediate onset.
  • Facial flushing, fever with or without chills, headache.
  • Low back pain, from the kidneys in acute renal failure. Also abdominal, chest, flank pain.
  • Blood in the urine (hemoglobinuria).
  • Dyspnea, tachypnea.
  • Hypotension. Cardiac arrest, death.
  • Treat the shock: keep the BP up with IV colloids, such as albumin.
  • Blood samples from the site.
  • First voided urine. A Foley to monitor urine output.
  • Send the unit, tubing and filter to the lab.
Febrile non-hemolytic (FNHTR)Fever develops during the infusion, not from hemolysis.
  • Sudden chills and fever, headache, flushing, anxiety, vomiting, muscle pain.
  • Not immediate: the temperature may rise a degree over the hour.
  • No low back pain, no blood in the urine, no severe hemodynamic compromise. A sharp drop in BP points to a hemolytic reaction.
  • Antipyretic: acetaminophen.
  • Restart only if the provider orders it.
Mild allergicThe most common. Sensitization to donor WBCs, platelets or plasma proteins.
  • Flushing, itching, urticaria (hives).
  • Antihistamine (diphenhydramine) or corticosteroid, as ordered.
  • Mild and transient: may restart slowly, with the provider's order.
Anaphylactic and severe allergicSensitization to donor plasma proteins.
  • Anxiety, urticaria, dyspnea and wheezing.
  • Progressing to cyanosis, bronchospasm, hypotension, shock and cardiac arrest.
  • Treated as a code: CPR, oxygen, epinephrine.
  • Also antihistamines, corticosteroids, β2 agonists.
Bacterial or sepsisBacteria in the blood, as when it hangs too long.
  • Rapid onset of chills and high fever.
  • Vomiting and diarrhea.
  • Marked hypotension.
  • Blood culture. Return the blood bag to the lab.
  • Treat the septicemia: antibiotics, IV fluids, vasopressors.
Transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO)Too much blood volume: the patient cannot handle the excess fluid.
  • Cough and dyspnea.
  • Pulmonary congestion: crackles at the bases.
  • Headache, increased vital signs, distended neck veins.
  • Slow or stop the infusion.
  • Diuretics (furosemide), oxygen, morphine: they ease the workload on the heart.
  • Stat chest x-ray.
Only TACO may be slowed in place of stopped. Only a febrile or a mild allergic reaction may restart, and only on the provider's order.
