Anemia

Why Anemia Is a Big Deal

  • Low hemoglobin or decreased RBCs. RBCs (erythrocytes) carry oxygen on their hemoglobin.
  • Either the RBCs are lacking, after a large loss of blood volume, or the hemoglobin is.
  • Not enough oxygen circulates to feed the tissues, which survive on aerobic metabolism.

General Signs and Symptoms of All Types of Anemia

ResponseWhat Is HappeningSigns and Symptoms
Tissue hypoxia
  • Hypoxia-related symptoms develop if the anemia is severe.
  • Whole body: each tissue shows its own lack of oxygen. The GI tract cramps, the brain aches or goes lightheaded, the muscles cramp.
Fatigue, SOB, angina, intermittent claudication, muscle cramps, headache, lightheadedness, syncope, abdominal cramping, nausea.
Selective increase in tissue perfusion
  • Selective vasoconstriction shunts blood to the priority organs: brain, heart, lungs.
  • Skin is restricted first.
  • Kidneys next. They take about 20% of cardiac output, so they take a big hit: acute kidney injury.
  • GI system after that: blood flow to the gut is cut.
Pale skin and pale mucous membranes, cool to touch. Loss of urine output. Nausea, lack of appetite.
Increased cardiac output
  • Heart works harder to deliver oxygen.
  • Tachycardia first: the first compensatory mechanism of fluid volume deficit.
  • Vessels clamp down to raise the blood pressure. Stroke volume rises if it can, which is unlikely when volume has been lost.
  • Angina: the heart is working harder and may not be getting the oxygen it needs.
Increased HR, BP and SV. Angina. Severe, down the road: high-output cardiac failure, cardiomegaly.
Increased pulmonary function
  • Due to hypoxia: the respiratory rate rises to compensate.
  • Often what patients talk about most: getting from the bed to the chair uses all their energy.
Tachypnea. SOB, exertional or at rest.
Increased RBC function
  • Longer-term compensatory mechanism.
  • More erythropoietin is produced, so more RBCs are made.
  • Reticulocytes are not as effective as fully mature RBCs.
Stress reticulocytosis: an increased reticulocyte count.

Timing

All the symptoms of anemia are based on timing: how fast it came on, and whether the compensatory responses had time to kick in.

Sudden Loss

  • All of the symptoms, very quickly: short of breath immediately, headache, lightheadedness, altered level of consciousness.
  • Compensatory responses have not had time to kick in.
  • Example: trauma with liters of blood lost.

Slow Loss

  • May be sitting up and talking with the same H&H as the trauma patient, even a hemoglobin of 4 and a hematocrit of 12.
  • The body has had time to compensate.
  • Example: a slow GI bleed.

Mechanisms of Anemia

MechanismExamples
RBC productionChronic kidney disease. Radiation that has irradiated the bone marrow.
RBC destructionHemolytic anemia: an autoimmune problem, or the wrong blood transfusion.
Blood lossThe most common: post-op, trauma, GI bleed.

The signs and symptoms are essentially the same whatever the mechanism.

The Bleeding Patient

Identify the source and treat the cause.

SourceTreating the Cause
Chronic GI bleedSurgery to clip the bleed. Or, on a blood thinner, reverse it and give product.
TraumaThe OR, to clamp and sew off the bleeding.
Chronic kidney diseaseErythropoietin supplementation.
Bone marrow not workingMedications to replace what is lost. A change to the radiation, or bone marrow replacement.

Coagulopathies

  • Abnormalities in one of the stages of clotting: the clotting cascade is altered.
  • Bleeding patients often develop one, and very quickly when actively bleeding, even on no anticoagulation. Seen in surgery and trauma.
  • Temperature plays a big role in clotting, which is why it is regulated in trauma and surgery.
  • Examine the bleeding times and coags of every bleeding patient.
  • No known reason for the bleeding: think of an inherited coagulopathy (hemophilia, von Willebrand syndrome) or an acquired one (a medication, chemo).
  • Corrected with vitamin K or a factor VII drug, usually as a one-time dose. Given quite a bit in trauma and post-op recovery.
What is concerning: perfusion to the vital organs. No urine output in 6 or 7 hours matters more than pale, cool skin, which is an expected finding.

Medications and the Bleeding Patient

  • Always know what medications the patient is on.
  • Bright red blood in the sputum from an ET tube, on a heparin drip: does the drip need to stop?
  • GI bleed with a history of atrial fibrillation: is the patient on warfarin or apixaban?
  • Known to be bleeding after surgery, with an enoxaparin order: question it. It is sometimes still given, because the risk of DVT is so high.

Why it matters: active bleeding can lead to hypovolemic shock, which is low blood volume shock.

What Can Affect Coagulation

FactorHow It Affects Coagulation
Alcohol, poisons, substancesAll can affect bleeding times and coagulation. High levels of ethanol make the blood thinner.
Blood clots (clotting disorders)A history of blood clots is a huge risk for more, and a reason to worry about a clotting disorder.
Liver diseaseThe liver makes a lot of the clotting factors, so bleeding times are prolonged.
Active bleeding, especially with a lot of lossAffects the coagulation cascade. Plasma and clotting factors often need replacing as well.
Cardiac bypassA lot of blood thinner is given to go on bypass. Blood touching what it normally does not can develop a coagulopathy. The longer on bypass, the more risk.
SplenectomyHigher risk for bleeding issues.
Radiation, for cancer or from exposureBone marrow is irradiated or suppressed, and it makes the platelets and RBCs. Very high risk.
Previous transfusionsA lot of transfusions means a lot more antibodies, and can also alter coagulation.
Infections and inflammatory statesInflammatory responses can disrupt coagulation, as in DIC. See below.

Labs

LabWhat to Know
CBC and H&H
  • Can be deceptive. The same H&H can belong to a patient who is talking and to one who is unresponsive, so H&H and CBC are trended, not read as one value.
  • H&H may not reflect the anemia accurately, because plasma is lost equally.
  • As bleeding continues, fluid shifts intravascularly and dilutes the remaining blood cells.
  • IV fluid resuscitation dilutes further. It can make the hematocrit seem lower than it is, so look at the hemoglobin as well.
  • Full extent of an acute bleed cannot be evaluated for at least 48 to 72 hours.
Reticulocyte count
  • Becomes elevated within several days, about as quickly as the kidneys can help.
Bleeding time (coags)
  • PT and PTT. INR, but not generally in an acute bleed.
  • Fibrinogen, a clotting product.
TEG (thromboelastogram)
  • Used to examine coagulopathy: the shape of the tracing tells which part of the clotting cascade is abnormal.

Interventions for the Critically Ill Bleeding Patient

InterventionWhat to Know
Assessment
  • Oxygenation, cardiac output, level of consciousness.
  • Skin, urine, stool and secretions, for evidence of active bleeding. Mucous membranes and pulmonary secretions too.
  • Dressings: bleeding into them can be insidious, and they may need to be weighed.
Transfuse
  • What products do they need? A TEG can show a need for something other than packed cells, or whether packed cells are needed at all.
Fluid resuscitation
  • A bleeding patient is volume depleted and eventually becomes hypotensive.
  • Blood is a finite resource, so fluid is given in addition.
Monitoring labs
  • Monitored closely.
Anticoagulants off
  • Off when appropriate: no heparin drip while actively bleeding.
  • Enoxaparin may still be needed, depending on the situation.
Bleeding precautions
  • Depend more on the platelet count than on the H&H.
  • A low H&H means the patient is actively bleeding, not necessarily at higher risk for bleeding.
Limit invasive procedures
  • Active bleeding or altered coagulation times: not the best time to place a long-term port.
  • A central line that is needed still goes in. Expect the site to bleed, and be ready for it.

Blood Products

ProductIncludesUsesInfusion Rate
Whole bloodRBCs, WBCs, platelets and plasma.
  • Severe trauma and blood loss.
  • Typically reserved for trauma, the ICU and the ED. Research is finding it very beneficial, so that may change.
As fast as the patient can tolerate, for severe blood loss.
Packed red blood cells (PRBCs)Concentrated RBCs, separated from the platelets and plasma.
  • Anemia, GI bleed, trauma or surgery.
  • Primarily what is replaced in a patient who is actively bleeding.
2 to 4 hours on average.
PlateletsCell fragments for blood clotting.
  • Low platelets, leukemia, chemotherapy.
  • Given only when the count is very low: usually under 50,000, 40,000 or even 20,000, depending on active bleeding.
30 to 60 minutes, faster if needed.
Fresh frozen plasma (FFP)Liquid portion of blood. Contains water, electrolytes, proteins and antibodies.
  • Clotting disorders (DIC, TTP), severe blood loss.
  • Replaces coagulation factors.
1 to 4 hours on average.
AlbuminPlasma protein. No clotting factors.
  • Shock, burns, ascites.
  • Increases blood volume: pulls fluid into the vascular space.
Individualized rate. Check hospital policy.
Cryoprecipitate (cryo)Derived from plasma. Has some clotting factors.
  • Helps stop bleeding.

In practice, platelets, plasma, cryo and albumin are often run wide open, unless the patient is at really high risk for pulmonary edema. PRBCs are different: start slow, typically over 2 hours, and done within 4.

What each product is, compatibility and giving PRBCs are taught in the Transfusions review

Mass Transfusion

1:1:1:1 ratio. One PRBC : one FFP : one platelet : one cryo. A mass transfusion is never just PRBCs.

Massive Transfusion Protocol (MTP)

  • For a patient who is bleeding to death. Rapid transfusion, by the hospital's or the unit's protocol.
  • Ordered by an MD, in coordination with a doctor from the blood bank.
  • Blood does not have to be cross-matched initially.
  • Why the ratio: PRBCs alone fall out of wherever the patient is bleeding. FFP, platelets and cryo help clot the bleed while the PRBCs fill the tank.
  • Fibrinogen may be especially important in obstetric hemorrhage. Supplemented with fibrinogen concentrates or cryoprecipitate. These patients usually receive tranexamic acid as well.
  • A Level 1 or a Belmont gives a whole unit of blood in minutes, and warms it.
  • Most products are very cold, and a hypothermic patient's blood does not clot as effectively. Warm the patient, give warm products, replace clotting factors, replace volume.

Thrombocytopenia and HIT

TopicWhat to Know
Platelet count
  • Thrombocytopenia is low platelets: technically under 150,000.
  • Under 30,000 is critically low, and a risk for a spontaneous bleed.
  • Transfusion usually waits until the count is much more critically low.
Causes
  • Usually a bone marrow issue: chemo, radiation, a bone marrow cancer.
  • Increased destruction of platelets, or their sequestration or abnormal distribution.
  • Also cardiac bypass surgery, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), sepsis, cancers.
  • Identify the cause and address it. HIT: stop the heparin.
Skin assessment
  • Petechiae, purpura, ecchymosis.
  • Oozing mucous membranes: bleeding from the nose, mouth and lips.
Where to look
  • Wounds, stool, urine, nose and pulmonary secretions: where a bleed is most likely to be evident.
Bleeding precautions
  • Needed most of the time, especially with a really low count.
Evaluate meds
  • Done by the team and pharmacy: nothing given should make a low count even lower. A medication that lowers platelets may need to stop, but sometimes it is necessary.
  • Some antibiotics, antidysrhythmics such as amiodarone, and NSAIDs can cause thrombocytopenia. Avoided in these patients, and a possible cause when none is known.
Labs
  • Serotonin release assay (SRA): detects platelet activation in the presence of antibodies, so it tells whether this is HIT. Technically challenging, and not easy to obtain.
  • Platelets trending down on a heparin drip: HIT is assumed.
  • Dropping platelets: off anticoagulation, usually, especially when the count is really low.
  • Coags, H&H and CBC are monitored.

Thrombocytopenic Precautions

  • Toothettes for oral care.
  • No flossing.
  • Electric shaver only.
  • Gentle nose blowing and coughing.
  • Avoid invasive procedures: enemas, rectal thermometers, suppositories, needle sticks.
  • No tourniquets.
  • No tampons.
  • Assess fall risk.
  • No aspirin or aspirin products.
  • Avoid constipation.

Needle sticks are often not avoidable at a higher level of care. A central line or an arterial line saves frequent sticks, but those lines are at high risk of bleeding and oozing. Pressure dressings, or special dressings for oozing areas, go on them.

DIC: Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation

DIC is an emergency. The patient is having microemboli (small clots) and is also bleeding. A very dangerous scenario.

Trigger

Vessel injury from a disease state, tissue injury or a foreign body in the bloodstream. Trauma, burns and sepsis are the most common.

Microvascular Clotting

Exaggerated coagulation. Uncontrolled microclots lodge in the small vessels: organ ischemia and necrosis.

Clotting Factors Depleted

The body also senses the clots and tries to thin the blood, but the clots have already lodged.

Bleeding

Really prolonged bleeding time. Any area that can bleed will bleed.

Clinical Picture and Diagnosis of DIC

  • An organ dies when microclots clog its circulation: liver, kidneys, lungs, heart.
  • Skin, lungs and kidneys are most commonly affected.
  • Manifests as thrombophlebitis, pulmonary embolism, stroke, GI bleeding and renal failure, often all at once.
  • Skin manifestations: sometimes only on the hands, the feet and the tips of the toes.
  • Generally acute and life threatening. A chronic low-grade DIC exists.
  • Diagnosis: by risk factors, presentation and lab values (coags).

Nursing Interventions for DIC

Goal: preserve organ function.

InterventionWhat to Know
Correct hypotension, hypoxemia, acidosis
  • Hypotension: low volume.
  • Hypoxemia: active blood loss means fewer circulating RBCs.
  • Acidosis: tissue ischemia related to organ death.
Treat the underlying cause
  • Trauma or sepsis: try to reverse it.
Replace clotting factors and platelets
  • Platelets are the highest priority: they provide the platelet plug at the beginning of the clotting cascade.
  • FFP replaces fibrinogen and other clotting factors.
Heparin
  • A potent thrombin inhibitor. In very low doses it blocks the clotting processes that initiate DIC and prevents further clotting.
  • Counterintuitive in a patient who might be actively bleeding.
Treat pain and address comfort
  • Very painful: organs and tissues are dying from the microclots.
  • Pain is treated whether the patient is awake or sedated.
Monitor labs
  • Monitored very closely.

Care of the Immunocompromised Patient

TopicWhat to Know
What it is
  • WBCs are either decreased or non-functioning.
  • Usually asymptomatic until an infection. The patient may not look very sick, but a lot could go wrong.
  • Congenital or acquired.
Who
  • Organ transplant, bone marrow transplant, chemo, AIDS.
Labs
  • CBC: the white blood cell count shows leukopenia.
  • CD4 counts, for a solid organ transplant or HIV and AIDS.
Infection
  • Watch for opportunistic infections, and for infections in general.
  • No normal white blood cell response, so the patient cannot mount a response to an infection.
  • Temperature: usually at the bottom of the list of vital signs, but not here. A very low white count makes a temp very concerning.
  • Infection and sepsis are the primary cause of death.
Precautions
  • Protective isolation or neutropenic precautions.
  • Masks worn all the time. A HEPA filter room may be needed.
  • Dietary restrictions, such as no raw fruits and vegetables.
Teaching for home
  • Wash fruits and vegetables. No raw fish or sushi. Cook meat thoroughly.
