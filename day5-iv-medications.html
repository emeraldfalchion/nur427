Continuous IV Medications

What Continuous IV Medications Are Used For

  • Pain control and sedation.
  • Blood pressure management.
  • Heart rhythm management.
  • Infection: antibiotics.
  • Fluids: to help the blood pressure, and to keep the patient hydrated.
  • TPN and lipids.

Nursing Responsibilities

ResponsibilityWhat to Check
Correct bag, labeled, active order
  • The correct bag and the correct concentration: many concentrations exist.
  • An active order, and everything matches the MAR.
  • At bedside report, first thing: see each drip's drug, concentration and rate for yourself. A rate changed at 6:30 can be missed in report.
  • Example: report says epinephrine at 0.04. Check it is epinephrine, the concentration is correct, and the rate is 0.04.
Labeled tubing
  • Labeled in two places.
  • When the set must be changed: a sticker on every line.
  • At the port: which med goes in where at the patient, so each line can be followed to the patient. Many drips often share many access points.
Changing out IV tubing
  • Every 72 hours at UK. Check the start and discard dates on the sticker.
  • No sticker: start a new line.
Line patency
  • The pump alarms when a line is occluded.
  • A line on KVO: flush it to make sure it is patent.
Site inspection
  • Follow the line back to the site, peripheral IV especially, central line too.
  • No hematoma, oozing, bleeding, signs of infection or infiltration. Every assessment.
Compatibility
  • Many continuous IV meds are not compatible with one another, and it is not always obvious.
  • The nurse's responsibility. Several meds often share one port, with a runner behind them.
  • Check the meds with each other and with the runner's IV fluid, at least once a shift.

Chart each drip's rate every hour, especially while titrating often. Many drips at once is common.

Titrating IV Medications

PrincipleWhat to Know
Understand standard dosing
  • What is typical to start at, and to increase by. It differs by drug and by unit.
  • Units: mcg/kg/min (most meds), mL/hr, mcg/min, units/min (vasopressin only).
  • Example: epinephrine starts at 0.02 mcg/kg/min and goes up by 0.02: 0.04, then 0.06. By 0.01 if the patient is tenuous.
Confirm the order
  • Usually a minimum and a maximum. Check the MAR and know the max.
  • Not every order is titratable: some say to leave the dose and not titrate. Amiodarone, for one.
  • Pressors maxed out: start another, or ask whether this is severe shock heading toward MODS.
  • Example: a titratable range of 0.02 to 0.4 mcg/kg/min.
Onset of working
  • How quickly will it work? Epinephrine has a very quick onset: the effect shows fast.
  • Not working: something else may be going on. Vessels can only be constricted so much without fluid on board.
Titrate slowly
  • Up and down, always slowly. Know the patient's condition, hemodynamics and goal.
  • Example: MAP 56, goal 60, epinephrine at 0.02. Go to 0.04, never to 0.1.

Sedation and Pain Medications

Why sedate: oxygen supply and demand, and keeping the patient cooperating with ventilatory support.

DrugClassKey Points
Fentanyl (mcg/hr)Opioid
  • The most common, with midazolam, as continuous drips.
Morphine (mg/hr)Opioid
  • Not very frequent as a drip. Seen more by PCA.
HydromorphoneOpioid
  • Can run as a drip. Seen more by PCA.
Midazolam (mg/kg/hr)Benzodiazepine
  • The most common sedation, with fentanyl.
  • An anxiolytic.
Lorazepam (mg/kg/hr)Benzodiazepine
  • An anxiolytic.
Propofol (mcg/kg/min)Other sedative
  • Very quick acting: for sedation fast, as when intubating or with a very high oxygen need.
  • Stops working very quickly: pause it a minute or two and the patient comes to. Good for sedation vacations and for testing breathing on the vent.
  • Fentanyl and midazolam stay in the system longer, especially with poor kidney function: mental status is harder to test quickly.
Dexmedetomidine (mcg/kg/hr)Other sedative
  • Used a lot with substance use disorder.
  • One of the only sedatives usable without intubation, though used mostly in intubated patients.
  • Helps with high levels of anxiety. Compromises respiration less than other sedation.

Considerations With IV Pain and Sedation Medications

ConsiderationWhat to Know
LOC
  • All pain and sedation meds depress the LOC.
  • A neuro status at least every 4 hours in the ICU: pause the pain and sedation meds a few minutes and check the LOC.
  • Some patients are too hemodynamically unstable to tolerate the pause.
Respiratory function
  • Compromised with the LOC.
  • Opioids, sedation and most sedatives need ventilatory support. A patient on pain and sedation drips is nearly always mechanically ventilated.
Blood pressure
  • These meds depress the BP. A balance: keep the patient sedated and the MAP above 60.
  • The patient may be on pressors too.
  • Turning up pain or sedation meds tanks the BP: monitor it closely.
Pain assessment
  • Thorough pain assessments with IV pain meds.
Bolus
  • For breakthrough pain: an order for a fentanyl bolus, or call for one.
  • Any bolus further depresses the LOC and the BP.
PCA
  • Common for pain meds in the ICU.

Paralytic Medications

Never paralyze until sedated. No push or drip of vecuronium or rocuronium until the patient is completely sedated. An airway must be in place: a paralyzed patient cannot move the diaphragm, so cannot breathe. Every paralyzed patient is mechanically ventilated.

Paralytics (Neuromuscular Blockade)

  • When: sedation alone (fentanyl and midazolam, or propofol) is not enough, because oxygen supply and demand are so unbalanced.
  • Common in: increased ICP, ARDS. Septic shock and others too.
  • Also: a patient not cooperating with mechanical ventilation.
  • Vecuronium, rocuronium: pushed or run as a drip by the nurse.
  • Succinylcholine, etomidate: quicker acting. Anesthesia pushes them, not nurses.

Train of Four

Train of Four: Monitoring the Paralytic

  • Monitors the paralytic effect. Paralytics are titrated by its twitches.
  • Get a baseline before sedating and paralyzing.
  • Placed on the wrist: watch the thumb twitch.
  • Chart it regularly, per hospital policy.

Caring for the Paralyzed Patient

  • Cannot do anything for themselves.
  • Skin and mucous membranes: oral care. Eye lubrication, since without muscle control the eyes may not close all the way.
  • Passive range of motion, often with PT: the effects of immobility.

Blood Pressure and Hemodynamic Management

Keeping the blood pressure up is the primary concern for perfusion. A hemodynamically unstable patient often needs meds to raise the BP or cardiac output, or alter the heart rate.

Fill the tank before you squeeze the hose. These meds are insanely effective, but cannot squeeze an empty tank. The patient is fluid resuscitated, with adequate preload, before a pressor goes on, especially in trauma resuscitation and sepsis. Fluid resuscitation often continues alongside the pressor.

How Would You Know Their Volume Status?

  • Invasive hemodynamic monitoring: a stroke volume, a calculated SVR.
  • Edematous? May need a colloid to pull the third spacing back into the vessel.
  • Intake and output. Urine output.
  • Dry mucous membranes, though a dry patient is harder to see.

Stroke volume and SVR are taught in Hemodynamics

Squeeze, Clamp, or Both

How Meds Raise the Blood Pressure

  • Squeeze: inotropes. The heart contracts harder, raising cardiac output and so the BP.
  • Clamp: vasoconstriction. The arteries' diameter gets smaller and tighter, raising the SVR.
  • Clamp and squeeze: a little of both. Many meds.
DrugActionEffectsMain Uses
Dobutamine, milrinoneSqueeze
  • Straight contractility: a great big impact on cardiac output.
  • Raise the heart rate. High risk of dysrhythmias.
  • Little effect on SVR (milrinone a bit more). Usually do not drop the BP much.
  • Cardiogenic shock: an ineffective heart muscle.
VasopressinClamp
  • Pure vasoconstriction: vastly raises the SVR and the BP.
  • May affect cardiac output, depending on how bad the SVR is.
  • Distributive shock, such as sepsis.
  • Pulmonary hypertension.
PhenylephrineClamp
  • Pure vasoconstriction.
  • Not commonly used. Sometimes trauma.
  • OB: postpartum hemorrhage, a BP drop from an epidural.
NorepinephrineClamp and squeeze
  • Mostly a clamper, with a little effect on cardiac output.
  • Most types of shock.
EpinephrineClamp and squeeze
  • Both: raises the SVR, with a significant effect on cardiac output.
  • Can affect the heart rate and rhythm.
  • All types of shock. The first stop in the CV ICU.
DopamineClamp and squeeze
  • Low dose: primarily for perfusion.
  • Medium and higher doses: impact cardiac output.
  • Lack of better agents: still failing on norepinephrine, vasopressin, epinephrine and phenylephrine. Not used much anymore.
Most of these are not safe for peripheral use. Epinephrine and norepinephrine need a central line most of the time.

Vasodilators

A High Blood Pressure: Before a Vasodilator

  1. Pain or anxiety? Make sure pain control is adequate.
  2. Meant to be sedated, for oxygen supply and demand? Make sure that is controlled.
  3. Then think of a vasodilator.

Vasodilators

  • Nitroprusside: immediate action. Works very, very fast.
  • Nicardipine: the one primarily used.
  • Nitroglycerin.

Cardiac Rhythm Management

Continuous Rhythm Control

  • Why: in shock, a perfusion problem leaves the heart short of oxygen and nutrients, so it is much more predisposed to dysrhythmias.
  • Examples: AFib with RVR, in and out of torsades, long runs of VTach.
  • Amiodarone: first line, continuous.
  • Lidocaine: second line, continuous.
  • Metoprolol: IV push, for rhythm control too.
