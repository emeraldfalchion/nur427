Hematologic

DiagnosisPriority ProblemsKey CuesHow the Pathophysiology Links
DICBleeding
  • Platelets falling fast: being used up.
  • PT and PTT prolonged, fibrinogen low: the clotting factors used up.
  • Oozing at IV sites and gums: bleeding from insertion sites and mucous membranes.
  • Petechiae, an ecchymosis: little bleeds under the skin.
  • Sepsis: mediators from the bacteria and white cells set off unregulated clotting. Platelets clump, and microclots form in the small vessels.
  • The clotting uses up the platelets and clotting factors, fibrinogen among them, and the body tries to thin the blood.
  • Unable to clot: any area that can bleed will bleed. Aspirin, an antiplatelet, adds to it, so it is held.
  • Below 30,000, the platelets put the patient at risk for a spontaneous bleed.
Ineffective peripheral tissue perfusion, with PAD
  • The tips of the toes dusky purple, mottled and cool.
  • Capillary refill slower in the toes than the fingers.
  • A new burning ache in the toes, unlike the claudication.
  • Weak pedal pulses and cool feet, with PAD: less flow to start with.
  • The same microclots lodge in the small vessels and cut off the flow behind them: ischemia, then necrosis.
  • DIC's skin signs often show first on the tips of the toes.
  • PAD: atherosclerosis in the leg arteries, and perfusion cannot get past it.
  • Sepsis opens the vessels wide, lowering the BP and the perfusion to the tissues. Dying tissue is very painful.
The bleeding patient and anemiaDeficient fluid volume
  • Tachycardia, the first compensation for lost volume.
  • A low BP that holds only after fluid.
  • Bright red blood in the drain and dressing, and a spreading ecchymosis: still bleeding.
  • Hemoglobin falling. The full loss does not show for 48 to 72 hours.
  • Urine output under 0.5 mL/kg/hr, with pale, cool skin.
  • Blood lost from the vessels leaves too little volume to fill them.
  • Tachycardia first, then the vessels clamp down to send blood to the brain, heart and lungs. The skin is restricted first, the kidneys next.
  • Stroke volume cannot rise with the volume gone.
  • The hemoglobin may not show the whole loss: plasma is lost with the cells, and IV fluid dilutes what is left.
  • Active bleeding can lead to hypovolemic shock.
Activity intolerance, with CKD
  • Standing: lightheaded, short of breath, tachycardic, nearly fainting.
  • Short of breath for minutes after a small move.
  • Weakness and fatigue.
  • A moderate anemia on top of a chronic one.
  • Fewer red cells: too little oxygen reaches the tissues.
  • At rest, a faster heart rate and breathing make up for it. Any effort asks for more oxygen than the blood can carry: getting from the bed to the chair uses all their energy.
  • CKD: the kidneys make less erythropoietin, so the marrow makes fewer red cells, and the patient was anemic before the bleed.
Thrombocytopenia and HITBleeding
  • Skin: petechiae, purpura, ecchymosis.
  • Oozing mucous membranes: the nose, mouth and lips.
  • Bleeding in wounds, stool, urine, the nose and pulmonary secretions.
  • Temperature, when chemo or the bone marrow is the cause: with a very low white count, any temp is very concerning.
  • Too few platelets for the platelet plug.
Infection, when chemo or the bone marrow is the cause
  • White blood cells decreased or not working: no normal response to an infection.
The immunocompromised patientInfection
  • Usually asymptomatic until an infection. May not look very sick.
  • Temperature: with a very low white count, any temp is very concerning.
  • Opportunistic pneumonia: fever, chills, cough, dyspnea, fatigue.
  • No normal white blood cell response.
Impaired gas exchange
  • An opportunistic pneumonia infects the lung parenchyma. With no normal white cell response, it can progress to respiratory distress, then respiratory failure.

Cardiovascular

DiagnosisPriority ProblemsKey CuesHow the Pathophysiology Links
Acute coronary syndrome (ACS)Decreased cardiac tissue perfusion
  • Chest pressure not relieved by rest or nitroglycerin: ischemic pain, the hallmark of an MI.
  • Troponin elevated: heart cells are dying.
  • ST depression and T wave inversion, no ST elevation: read with the troponin as an NSTEMI.
  • Tachycardia with PVCs: more oxygen needed, and ischemic conducting cells misfire.
  • Hypertension, hyperlipidemia and smoking build atherosclerotic plaque in the coronary arteries.
  • A plaque ruptures and a thrombus partly blocks a coronary artery. The myocardium past it gets no oxygen and starts to die.
  • A fast heart rate raises the demand while the supply is cut. Without reperfusion, the infarct grows.
Impaired gas exchange, with COPD
  • Low SpO2 on room air: hypoxemia.
  • Tachypnea, slightly labored.
  • Decreased breath sounds in the bases, expiratory wheezes, prolonged expiration, barrel chest: the COPD.
  • Emphysema has damaged the alveoli: less oxygen crosses into the blood, all the ischemic myocardium gets.
  • Hypoxemia severely impacts cardiac tissue and can set off rhythm changes.
  • Oxygen improves the heart's supply. The SpO2 target is 88 to 92%, as for any patient with COPD.
Acute heart failureImpaired gas exchange
  • Low SpO2 and PaO2: hypoxemia.
  • Tachypnea blowing off CO2: an uncompensated respiratory alkalosis.
  • Crackles with pink, blood-tinged sputum: pulmonary edema.
  • Sleeping upright, the tripod position, neck and thoracic muscles in use.
  • An old MI left a damaged myocardium that cannot contract well: a low ejection fraction.
  • Salty food raises the blood volume and the preload.
  • The weak left ventricle cannot pump it forward: blood backs up into the left atrium and the pulmonary veins, pulmonary congestion and edema.
  • Too little oxygen reaches the blood, and breathing speeds up to make up for it.
Electrolyte imbalance: a high potassium, with CKD
  • Potassium high.
  • Frequent PVCs: an irritable heart.
  • Creatinine up from baseline, with the BUN: the kidneys are excreting less.
  • Spironolactone at home: a potassium-sparing diuretic.
  • The kidneys are primarily responsible for excreting potassium.
  • CKD from diabetes and hypertension, and the failing heart sends the kidneys less blood: they take 20 to 25% of cardiac output.
  • Spironolactone spares potassium too.
  • A high potassium brings EKG changes and ventricular dysrhythmias that can be life-threatening, and each lowers the cardiac output further.
Aortic aneurysmDeficient fluid volume
  • Usually asymptomatic until it ruptures. Sometimes back or abdominal pain with a AAA.
  • Ruptured: very hypovolemic, chest pain, diaphoretic.
  • A rupture bleeds out.
Ineffective tissue perfusion
  • Blood flow lost below the rupture.
Cardiac tamponadeDecreased cardiac output
  • Beck's triad: low BP, JVD, muffled heart sounds.
  • After surgery: chest tube output stops abruptly, with sudden hypotension and tachycardia.
  • Blood in the pericardial space keeps the heart from beating effectively.
PericarditisAcute pain
  • Pericardial friction rub: the hallmark.
  • Very painful: mimics MI pain. Dyspnea, fatigue, anxiety.
Decreased cardiac output
  • It can cause a tamponade.
Valve disease and infective endocarditisDecreased cardiac output
  • Valve disease: heart failure signs, exertional dyspnea, fatigue, chest pain, a murmur.
  • Endocarditis: a really sick infection. High temp, chills, diaphoresis, low BP, dysrhythmias.
  • The blood backs up behind the valve.
Hyperthermia, in endocarditis
  • Bacteria in the bloodstream lodge on the valve as vegetations.
AV blocksDecreased cardiac output
  • Bradycardia: the biggest concern.
  • Compromised cardiac output, more symptomatic with each degree.
  • The rate is too slow.

Renal

DiagnosisPriority ProblemsKey CuesHow the Pathophysiology Links
Acute kidney injuryDeficient fluid volume
  • Hypotension with tachycardia: what a pre-renal AKI looks like.
  • Oliguria: under 0.5 mL/kg/hr, dark and concentrated.
  • Weak, thready pulses, slow capillary refill, cool and clammy skin, flat neck veins, dry mucous membranes, weight lost.
  • Creatinine up from baseline with the BUN: both up, so the kidneys. Creatinine is the most reliable indicator. Dehydration gives the BUN a false high.
  • Usually multifactorial: most often pre-renal plus intrarenal, as here. Sepsis is the most common cause in the critically ill.
  • Vomiting and diarrhea empty the vessels, and the BP falls. The kidneys take 20 to 25% of cardiac output: hypoperfusion, a pre-renal cause.
  • Contrast dye while very dehydrated: a nephrotoxic drug in a fluid volume deficit, a double punch. Direct nephron injury, an intrarenal cause, on top.
  • CKD carries a much higher risk for AKI.
  • Rehydrated early, with the cause solved, an AKI can reverse.
Electrolyte imbalance: a high potassium, with CKD
  • Potassium high.
  • Tall, peaked T waves: a potassium imbalance, showing on the EKG.
  • A partially compensated metabolic acidosis: the kidneys failing to keep bicarb, the lungs compensating with hyperventilation.
  • Stage 3A CKD: the kidneys already excrete less.
  • The kidneys are primarily responsible for excreting potassium. In CKD it is one of the first electrolytes to become abnormal.
  • Almost no urine: the potassium builds up.
  • Failing kidneys cannot keep bicarb or excrete acid. In a metabolic acidosis, H+ moves into the cells and K+ comes out.
  • Hyperkalemia slows depolarization. Above 6.0 come dysrhythmias that can be life-threatening.
Pre-renal AKIDeficient fluid volume
  • Hypotension, with tachycardia.
  • Hypotensive or hypovolemic for a while: the AKI may develop within the next day or two.
  • Too little volume to perfuse the kidneys.
Decreased cardiac output, when heart failure is the cause
  • Low cardiac output from heart failure: hypoperfusion of the kidneys.
Intrarenal AKIExcess fluid volume
  • Hypertension for a short time, as in ATN, from RAAS malfunction.
  • A nephrotoxic drug or contrast dye in the history.
  • Injured nephrons cannot excrete the fluid.
Electrolyte imbalance: a high potassium
  • No longer excreted.
Post-renal AKIImpaired urinary elimination
  • None taught.
  • Urine cannot get past the blockage.
Electrolyte imbalance: a high potassium
  • No longer excreted.

GI and Hepatic

DiagnosisPriority ProblemsKey CuesHow the Pathophysiology Links
Acute pancreatitisDeficient fluid volume
  • Hypotension with tachycardia: compensating for too little volume in the vessels.
  • Repeated vomiting, nothing kept down: fluid lost, and none taken in.
  • Weak pulses, slow capillary refill, dry and sticky mucous membranes.
  • BUN and creatinine up: the kidneys are getting less blood.
  • Biliary disease: one of the two most common causes.
  • Enzymes leak into the pancreas's own tissue, and it digests itself. The dying cells set off the inflammatory response.
  • Fluid collects in the retroperitoneal space: up to a third of the plasma volume. Vomiting and a lost appetite add to it.
  • Shock and hypovolemia: the primary cause of death in acute pancreatitis.
Unstable blood glucose, with type 2 diabetes
  • Hyperglycemia.
  • Type 2 diabetes: the glucose already hard to control.
  • Lipase very high, with fever and tachycardia: a severe case, which can swing the glucose low.
  • Pancreatitis can cause hyperglycemia, and diabetes already makes the glucose hard to control.
  • The pancreas makes insulin, and its inflamed, dying cells release less of it.
  • Severe enough, pancreatitis can cause hypoglycemia instead.
  • Poor glycemic control varies oxygen consumption in a body under stress.
GI bleed, including varicealDeficient fluid volume
  • Bright red hematemesis and lavage: actively bleeding. Melena: older blood.
  • Tachycardia first, compensating. Then the BP falls.
  • Pale, cool and clammy, slow capillary refill, weak pulses: getting paler.
  • Hemoglobin falling. BUN up with a normal creatinine: blood metabolized in the GI tract.
  • A past ulcer: a very high risk of another. NSAIDs are the most common drug cause, and an anticoagulant vastly increases the bleeding risk.
  • Blood lost from the vessels: a fluid volume deficit. The heart rate rises first, then the BP falls.
  • The hematocrit can lag for hours behind the loss.
Decreased cardiac tissue perfusion, with heart failure
  • Chest tightness: angina.
  • Low hemoglobin: too few red cells to carry oxygen.
  • Tachycardia: a faster heart needs more oxygen.
  • Short of breath walking, fainted at home.
  • An acute GI bleed looks like anemia: weakness, shortness of breath, angina, syncope.
  • Too little hemoglobin carries too little oxygen to the tissues, the myocardium included.
  • A heart weakened by heart failure beats faster for the lost volume: it needs more oxygen while it gets less.
  • A very severe bleed brings EKG changes.
Hepatic failureAcute confusion
  • Decreased level of consciousness: oriented to self only, drowsy and irritable.
  • Asterixis: the hands flap when held out.
  • Ammonia high: hepatic encephalopathy. Above 150, a raised risk of cerebral edema.
  • No lactulose and no bowel movement: the ammonia stays in the gut.
  • Cirrhosis, then a binge and days of acetaminophen: the biggest cause of acute liver failure, especially with binge drinking.
  • A failing liver cannot detoxify. Ammonia builds up, crosses the blood-brain barrier and depresses the brain.
  • Hepatic encephalopathy raises the risk of seizures. A falling level of consciousness puts the airway at risk.
Bleeding
  • INR above 1.5: slow to clot.
  • Platelets low: thrombocytopenia, under 150,000.
  • New bruises, and the gums ooze.
  • Esophageal varices.
  • The liver makes clotting factors II, V, VII, IX and X. A failing liver makes too few: prolonged bleeding times.
  • With low platelets too, the body cannot correct a bleed on its own.
  • Portal hypertension engorges the esophageal veins into varices. A bleeding varix without clotting factors: a critical care emergency.
Ischemic bowelIneffective GI tissue perfusion
  • Abdominal pain, especially after eating, then at baseline.
  • Urgent, loose stools. Bloody stool with dark red clots. Nausea, vomiting.
  • Fever, from dead gut.
  • The bowel wall gets no oxygenated blood, and dies.
Deficient fluid volume
  • Fluid shifting: often intravascularly depleted.
Bowel obstruction and paralytic ileusDysfunctional GI motility
  • Distension: the hallmark. Cramping periumbilical pain in waves.
  • Large-volume bilious vomiting. No flatus.
  • Bowel sounds: high-pitched and tinkling in a mechanical SBO, absent to hypoactive in an ileus.
  • Peristalsis is lost or blocked.
Deficient fluid volume
  • Vomiting, and no PO intake.
Abdominal compartment syndromeIneffective tissue perfusion
  • Hugely distended, possibly rigid: hard from the fluid.
  • Organs lose their blood flow as the pressure rises.
  • Fluid presses on the abdominal organs.
Acute pain
  • Very painful, and pain raises the abdominal pressure.

Neuro

DiagnosisPriority ProblemsKey CuesHow the Pathophysiology Links
TBI with increased ICPIneffective cerebral tissue perfusion
  • GCS falling from the normal 15.
  • A pounding headache and vomiting: signs of increased ICP in an awake patient.
  • CT: a subdural hematoma, a contusion and cerebral edema, more volume inside the skull.
  • An acceleration and deceleration injury: two impacts, two areas of the brain.
  • The contusion is the primary injury. The hematoma and the swelling are the secondary injury, and they keep growing.
  • Brain, blood and CSF share a skull that cannot give: as they expand, the ICP rises.
  • CPP = MAP − ICP: a rising ICP leaves less blood reaching the brain, the primary driver of more secondary injury.
Seizures, with epilepsy
  • Epilepsy, and a seizure today despite the anti-seizure medication.
  • A seizure seen: stiffening and jerking, a bitten tongue, incontinence.
  • Blood on the brain from the subdural hematoma.
  • A seizure disorder already makes seizures likely. Any TBI raises the risk: blood on the brain irritates the tissue.
  • A seizure greatly increases the ICP and the brain's metabolic demand, and could cost the airway.
StrokeIneffective cerebral tissue perfusion
  • Sudden one-sided weakness: facial droop, arm and leg weakness, slurred speech, from the opposite side of the brain.
  • NIHSS: a moderate stroke.
  • CT without contrast: no bleeding, so ischemic.
  • A clear time of onset, inside the window for tPA. A TIA 2 weeks before: the warning.
  • Hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes and smoking build plaque in the large arteries: the biggest risk factor for an ischemic stroke.
  • A clot on a ruptured plaque blocks an artery, and the brain it feeds loses its blood supply. Brain cells survive about 3 to 4 minutes without oxygen.
  • The penumbra around the dying core can still be saved if blood flow returns soon.
  • Dying neurons release glutamate, lactic acid builds, and cytotoxic edema injures more cells.
Aspiration, with an earlier stroke
  • Drooling from the corner of the mouth, a weak gag reflex.
  • A wet, gurgling voice after swallowing, with a weak cough.
  • Slurred speech: weak muscles of the mouth and throat.
  • Coughing on thin liquids since the first stroke.
  • A stroke impairs motor function, swallowing and the gag reflex included. The first stroke had already weakened the swallow.
  • Saliva or fluid slips into the trachea and lungs, and aspiration may be silent.
  • Dysphagia and a decreased level of consciousness: both risk factors for aspiration pneumonia. A suspected stroke is NPO.
Spinal cord injuryIneffective tissue perfusion: the spinal cord
  • MAP well below the goal of 85 to 90 for spinal cord perfusion.
  • Sinus bradycardia.
  • Temperature low: an injury at T6 or higher cannot regulate body temperature.
  • A C6 injury, no movement, sensation or reflexes below it: neurogenic shock is more likely at T6 or higher.
  • Autonomic pathways disrupted below the injury: neurogenic shock. Without sympathetic input the vessels dilate, a distributive shock.
  • The BP falls, the heart rate slows, and heat is lost.
  • A swelling cord with a low BP: ischemia, more secondary injury, a permanent loss of function. So the MAP is kept at 85 to 90 for about 7 days.
Ineffective breathing pattern, with asthma
  • Fast, shallow breaths with the diaphragm, the chest wall barely moving, neck muscles in use.
  • An uncompensated respiratory acidosis: shallow breaths not blowing off CO2.
  • Hypoxemia.
  • A weak cough, and expiratory wheezes from the asthma.
  • At C5 to C6 the diaphragm usually still works, but the intercostal and abdominal muscles are weak: shallow breaths, a cough that cannot clear secretions.
  • Any injury at T6 or above: a very high risk for breathing problems.
  • Asthma narrows the airways, adding work the weak muscles cannot meet.
  • Swelling that climbs toward the diaphragm's nerves could stop the breathing altogether.
MeningitisHyperthermia
  • Headache, stiff neck and vomiting, over 1 to 2 days.
  • Fever, a throbbing headache, nuchal rigidity.
  • Infection of the meninges and the CSF.
Ineffective cerebral tissue perfusion
  • The inflammation can cause cerebral edema and increased ICP.
SeizuresInjury
  • A decreased neurologic status with no clear cause.
  • Status epilepticus: a seizure longer than 5 minutes, or repeated seizures over 30 minutes.
Ineffective airway clearance
