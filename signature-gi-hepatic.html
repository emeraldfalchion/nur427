The GI and Hepatic Diagnoses

Acute pancreatitis
  • Alcohol or biliary disease: the most common causes.
  • I GET SMASHED: also trauma, steroids, hypercalcemia, hypertriglyceridemia, ERCP, drugs.
  • Severe pancreatitis is what leads to critical care.
  • Epigastric pain radiating to the flank or back, constant and dull, worse lying supine.
  • Guarding, distension, heavy nausea and vomiting, quick dehydration.
  • Fever, tachycardia, jaundice, restlessness. Cullen's sign and Grey Turner's sign.
  • Lipase: the most diagnostic, trended, with amylase.
  • A low calcium within 48 hours: very high risk for severe problems.
  • Albumin, for fluid status. Glucose, potassium, magnesium, CRP.
  • Deficient fluid volume: fluid collects in the retroperitoneal space, up to a third of the plasma volume.
  • Unstable blood glucose: hyperglycemia, or hypoglycemia when severe.
  • Acute pain: a general concern.
  • Starts localized, then can accelerate to a systemic inflammatory state.
  • Hypovolemic shock: the primary cause of death.
  • Necrotizing or hemorrhagic pancreatitis, a pseudocyst, abdominal compartment syndrome.
  • Rest the GI tract: NPO. An NG tube. TPN when severe.
  • Fluid replacement: usually colloids, such as albumin. LR. Potassium, calcium and magnesium replaced.
  • Glucose closely: an insulin or a dextrose drip. Pain management, quite a bit.
  • ARDS: pancreatitis is one of the biggest risk factors. Hypoxemia, pleural effusions.
  • AKI, from the fluid shifting. Pericardial effusion, DIC, sepsis, decreased LOC.
GI bleed, including variceal
  • A peptic ulcer before: very high risk of another, especially on an anticoagulant.
  • Critically ill: stress ulcers. A Mallory-Weiss tear after aggressive vomiting.
  • Varices: end-stage liver disease or acute liver failure, from portal hypertension.
  • Tachycardia first, then a falling BP, a rising RR and a falling urine output.
  • Getting paler. Bright red stool or emesis: active. Dark or melena: older.
  • Looks like anemia: weakness, shortness of breath, angina, syncope.
  • H&H every 1 to 2 hours. The hematocrit may not change for several hours.
  • BUN up with no kidney cause: blood metabolized in the GI tract. Lactate, clotting profiles, LFTs.
  • Endoscopy finds the bleed. Gastric lavage: active, old or stopped?
  • Deficient fluid volume: blood lost from the vessels.
  • Decreased cardiac tissue perfusion: too little hemoglobin to carry oxygen, with angina.
  • Aspiration in a variceal bleed: the airway comes first.
  • Hypovolemic shock, from the fluid volume deficit.
  • Massive rebleeding, such as the day after an endoclip.
  • Variceal: rapidly hemodynamically unstable, and quickly in hypovolemic shock.
  • Stabilize the vital signs: isotonic fluids, transfusions guided by the hemoglobin. Vasopressors only once the tank is full.
  • Endoscopy for an upper bleed. Surgery for massive rebleeding. A continuous pantoprazole drip.
  • Variceal: the airway first. Octreotide or vasopressin, banding, TIPS, a tamponade tube: never clamp it.
  • Kidneys and liver lose perfusion: BUN, creatinine, LFTs.
  • EKG changes from lack of blood flow, in a very severe bleed.
  • Clotting factors used up: FFP may be needed. In liver disease, blood in the gut drives up the ammonia.
Hepatic failure
  • Acetaminophen overdose: the biggest cause, especially with binge drinking.
  • Binge drinking, hepatotoxic drugs, a virus, a loss of perfusion, HELLP syndrome.
  • Chronic liver disease exacerbating, or damage to a healthy liver.
  • Neurologic, from lost detoxification: decreased LOC, irritability, convulsions, coma. Asterixis.
  • Jaundice, a sudden high fever, nausea and vomiting, lethargy.
  • Bleeding: from falls, GI bleeds, varices. Ascites.
  • Ammonia: the level of hepatic encephalopathy. Above 150, a risk of cerebral edema.
  • INR above 1.5, platelets below 150,000, low fibrinogen.
  • Albumin, bilirubin, liver enzymes. A liver biopsy when the cause is unknown: very dangerous.
  • Acute confusion: toxins such as ammonia build up and depress the brain.
  • Bleeding: the failing liver makes too few clotting factors.
  • Excess fluid volume: albumin production falls, and fluid third spaces.
  • Hepatic encephalopathy, grades 1 to 4. Grades 3 and 4: worse outcomes.
  • Cerebral edema: life-threatening. Ammonia above 200: brain herniation.
  • Bleeding, GI and variceal. Hypoglycemia.
  • Lactulose, titrated to 2 to 4 bowel movements a day and the neurologic status.
  • Coagulopathy: vitamin K, platelets when very low, FFP when bleeding with an INR above 1.5.
  • Focused neurologic assessments: seizures and the airway. Fluid status.
  • AKI. Infection, a higher risk.
  • Cardiopulmonary: the hyperdynamic state, then acute, severe heart failure (cardiohepatic syndrome).
Ischemic bowel
  • Low blood pressure: the gut takes the hit first. A clot or blockage in an artery.
  • Atherosclerosis, age over 50, smoking, heart failure and AFib, clotting disorders.
  • High-dose vasopressors, especially norepinephrine.
  • Abdominal pain, especially after eating, then at baseline.
  • Urgent, loose stools. Bloody stool with dark red clots. Nausea, vomiting.
  • Fever, from dead gut.
  • Lactate increasing: trended very frequently. WBC usually elevated.
  • Colonoscopy, or a barium study or CT with contrast. Not seen on an X-ray.
  • Ineffective GI tissue perfusion: the bowel wall gets no oxygenated blood, and dies.
  • Deficient fluid volume: fluid shifting, often intravascularly depleted.
  • The infarction can spread: a lactate that keeps rising means the gut is still dying.
  • A paralytic ileus, abdominal compartment syndrome.
  • Bowel rest. Fluid resuscitation.
  • Surgery into healthy pink bowel, often ending in a colostomy.
  • Treat the cause. Trend the lactate.
  • None taught.
Bowel obstruction and paralytic ileus
  • Mechanical SBO: a tumor, adhesion or hernia. Large bowel: usually a neoplasm.
  • Paralytic ileus, common in the ICU: opioids and anesthesia, GI surgery, ischemia or shock, peritonitis.
  • Distension: the hallmark. Cramping periumbilical pain in waves.
  • Large-volume bilious vomiting. No flatus.
  • Bowel sounds: high-pitched and tinkling in a mechanical SBO, absent to hypoactive in an ileus.
  • X-ray (KUB) or CT: dilated loops of small bowel, a collapsed colon.
  • Electrolytes: the small bowel absorbs nutrients and vitamins.
  • Dysfunctional GI motility: peristalsis is lost or blocked.
  • Deficient fluid volume: vomiting, and no PO intake.
  • Partial to complete: complete is more severe.
  • A severe ileus or obstruction can cause abdominal compartment syndrome.
  • NPO, fluid resuscitation, an NG tube, electrolytes, metoclopramide. Usually surgery for a mechanical SBO.
  • Avoid opioids and anesthesia when possible.
  • Ileus: moving, an aggressive bowel regimen, acetaminophen or NSAIDs for pain.
  • None taught.
Abdominal compartment syndrome
  • Massive fluid resuscitation: trauma, shock.
  • Third spacing: liver disease, heart failure, pancreatitis.
  • Abdominal surgery, a severe ileus, ischemic bowel, a ruptured aortic aneurysm.
  • Hugely distended, possibly rigid: hard from the fluid.
  • Organs lose their blood flow as the pressure rises.
  • Bladder pressure gives the IAP: 16 to 20 mmHg organ ischemia, above 20 multiple organ dysfunction.
  • Labs: none taught.
  • Ineffective tissue perfusion: fluid presses on the abdominal organs.
  • Acute pain: very painful, and pain raises the abdominal pressure.
  • IAP 16 to 20 mmHg: organ ischemia. Above 20: multiple organ dysfunction.
  • HOB completely flat: even reverse Trendelenburg raises the pressure.
  • Sedation and paralytics: pain and fighting the ventilator raise the pressure.
  • Surgical decompression: the abdomen left open. Antibiotics.
  • Multiple organ dysfunction: impaired blood flow to multiple organs.
  • An open abdomen: peritonitis, sepsis, septic shock, as bacteria translocate out of the gut.

The Patient

Histories, Vital Signs and Medications

HPI: Linda Morales is a 56-year-old Hispanic woman brought to the ED by her husband at 0300. Yesterday at 1600 she felt pain in her upper middle abdomen. It grew severe over the evening and now bores through to her back. It is constant, dull and aching, and worse when she lies flat: she can bear it only curled on her side. She has vomited 8 times since 1800 and cannot keep down sips of water. She feels thirsty and lightheaded when she stands. She denies chest pain, shortness of breath, blood in her vomit, black stools and diarrhea. Her gallstone pain in the past came in short bouts and eased within a few hours. This pain has not eased in 11 hours.

PMH: Gallstones with biliary colic, 2 years, waiting for elective gallbladder surgery. Type 2 diabetes, 9 years. Hypertriglyceridemia, 6 years. Hypertension, 12 years: her BP usually runs in the 140s over 80s.

SH: Married, lives with her husband in a one-story house. School bus driver. Never smoked. A glass of wine on holidays only. No drug use.

FH: Mother had gallstones and type 2 diabetes. Father died of an MI at 70.

Admission VS: BP 100/62, HR 124 (sinus tachycardia), RR 24, SpO2 95% on room air, temperature 38.2 °C (100.8 °F), pain 9/10.

Home medications:

  • Metformin, for diabetes
  • Lisinopril, for hypertension

Allergies: Latex (hives).

Priority nursing problem

  • Unstable blood glucose
Supporting cues
  • BP 100/62, down from her usual 140s, with HR 124: tachycardia compensating for too little volume in her vessels.
  • Vomited 8 times, nothing kept down since 1600: fluid lost, and none taken in.
  • Radial pulses 1+ and weak, capillary refill 4 seconds, dry and sticky mucous membranes.
  • BUN 30 and creatinine 1.2: her kidneys are getting less blood.
  • Glucose 338: hyperglycemia.
  • Type 2 diabetes for 9 years: her glucose was already hard to control.
  • Lipase 2,850 with fever and tachycardia: a severe case, which can swing her glucose low instead.
Relevance to the diagnosis
  • Biliary disease is one of the two most common causes of acute pancreatitis. Her pancreatic enzymes leaked into the pancreas's own tissue, and it began digesting itself. The dying cells set off the inflammatory response, and fluid is collecting in her retroperitoneal space: pancreatitis can sequester as much as a third of the plasma volume. Her heavy vomiting and lost appetite dehydrate her quickly on top of it. Shock and hypovolemia are the primary cause of death in acute pancreatitis.
  • Pancreatitis can cause hyperglycemia, and her diabetes already makes her glucose hard to control. The pancreas makes insulin, and its inflamed, dying cells release less of it. Severe enough, pancreatitis can cause hypoglycemia instead, so her glucose can swing either way. Poor glycemic control varies oxygen consumption in a body already under stress. She needs close monitoring and an insulin drip.
Concern rating
  • 2, urgent. The fluid keeps shifting, and hypovolemic shock is the primary cause of death. She is alert, compensating, and her systolic BP is above 90: she is not expected to die within the hour.
  • 2, urgent. A glucose this high, or a swing low, must be caught quickly. She is alert and talking, with no sign of a low glucose yet.

A Complication in Another System

PartAnswer
ComplicationAcute respiratory distress syndrome, ARDS (respiratory). The morning of her third day in the ICU.
Why it could occurPancreatitis is one of the biggest risk factors for ARDS, through its systemic inflammatory response. Inflammatory mediators in her bloodstream injure the vessels of other organs, and in her lungs they damage the alveolar-capillary membrane. Fluid, protein and blood cells leak into the alveoli: noncardiogenic pulmonary edema.
Priority cuesSpO2 86% on 6 L/min by nasal cannula, up from 2 L yesterday, not rising as the oxygen goes up: refractory hypoxemia. PaO2 52 on the ABG: under 60, hypoxemia. RR 34 and dyspneic: tachypnea. Fine crackles in the posterior and lateral fields to the mid lungs bilaterally, absent yesterday. Restless again. Chest x-ray: both lungs whited out.
ActionsDependent: a high-flow nasal cannula per order, set up by respiratory therapy, its oxygen turned up toward an SpO2 above 90% and a PaO2 above 60. A repeat ABG in 1 hour. Independent: sit her upright with the airway open, and minimize her oxygen demand: no bath, no turning she does not need, care clustered. Her SpO2 is watched continuously and a fall reported at once.
