Futile vs Potentially Inappropriate Interventions

Medically Futile

  • Cannot accomplish the intended physiologic goal. Will not work, however much we want it to.
  • Ask: can the treatment accomplish what it is intended to? No means futile.
  • Example: CPR on a patient dead for an hour. It will not restore circulation.

Potentially Inappropriate

  • May work physiologically, with some chance of the effect the patient seeks.
  • But: the burdens may outweigh the benefits, or it does not fit the prognosis, goals or ethical principles.
  • Example: dialysis for a permanent, severe anoxic brain injury. The dialysis works. Should it be provided?
Futile: it won't work. Potentially inappropriate: it might work, but there is disagreement over whether the benefit justifies the burdens.

The Nurse's Role in These Questions

  • Not to decide independently.
  • Advocate for the patient.
  • Take part in goals of care discussions.
  • Help clarify the patient's wishes, and collaborate with the team and the family.

End-of-Life Documents

DNR: Do Not Resuscitate

  • What it is: a provider's medical order. No CPR if the patient has a cardiac or respiratory arrest.
  • Means: no chest compressions, no defibrillation, no ACLS.
  • Does not mean stopping care: medications, treatment and nursing care go on. Antibiotics, surgery, dialysis, blood products are all still possible.
  • Applies only if the patient arrests.
  • Nursing: verify the code status at the start of each shift. Know the patient's wishes and the facility policy.

DNI: Do Not Intubate

  • What it is: if the respiratory status worsens, no endotracheal tube.
  • Means: no invasive mechanical ventilation.
  • Does not mean don't treat: oxygen, high flow, BiPAP and CPAP are still possible, by the patient's wishes.

Separate decisions: a patient can be DNR, DNI, both or neither, by the patient's preference.

Advance Directive

  • A legal document: the person's health care wishes, written in advance in case they cannot make decisions later.
  • Can include: treatment preferences, goals of care, end-of-life wishes, a health care surrogate or decision maker.
  • On admission, ask whether the patient has one. If so, get a copy for the medical record and tell the provider.
  • None: tell the provider, and ask whether the patient wants help completing one.

Palliative Care and Comfort Care

Palliative Care

  • Goal: relieve symptoms, reduce suffering, improve quality of life in a serious illness.
  • Who: the patient does not have to be dying. Not the same as hospice or end-of-life care.
  • Alongside cure: given with curative or life-prolonging treatment.
  • Common symptoms: pain, anxiety, dyspnea, nausea, confusion.
  • Frequent symptom assessment: a symptom not recognized cannot be treated. Ask about pain, assess breathing, watch for anxiety, agitation, a change in cognition.
  • Pain management: often a major part, but palliative care is much more than pain meds.
  • Patient and family support: listening, answering questions, simply being present.
  • Example: a patient on chemotherapy for cancer, given palliative care for pain, nausea and anxiety.

Comfort Care

  • Goal: maximize comfort, preserve dignity, the best possible quality of life.
  • Who: a patient whose goals of care are no longer centered on a cure.
  • Instead of cure: the priority shifts from curing disease to relieving suffering. Not stopping care: some of the most attentive nursing care.
  • Distressing symptoms: pain, dyspnea, anxiety, agitation, nausea.
  • Dyspnea in the actively dying: the goal is to reduce the sensation of shortness of breath, not to raise the oxygen saturation.
  • Comfort measures: repositioning, oral care, hygiene, moisturizing the lips, clean linens, protecting the skin.
  • A calm, peaceful environment: dim the lights, cut unnecessary alarms and interruptions, allow more family, privacy and dignity.
  • Also: emotional, spiritual and family needs. Carry out the patient's wishes and goals of care.
Comfort care does not mean "doing nothing." It means focusing on comfort rather than cure.

The Comfort Care Order Set

What a Comfort Care Order Set Holds

  • Vital signs every 8 hours: assessments are reduced, not eliminated. Nursing care stays very active.
  • Scheduled oral care: twice daily and as needed. A dry mouth is very uncomfortable.
  • Call the organ procurement organization: to consider donation after cardiac death. Hospital policy usually sets when.
  • Remove devices not needed for comfort: monitors, blood pressure cuffs, leg compression devices. All ICU devices, such as a cardiac output computer or an intra-aortic balloon pump.
  • Verify the DNR: updated in the chart and signed by the attending physician.
  • Music therapy consult: for quality of life and comfort.
  • Antiemetics for nausea.
  • Meds for oral secretions: see below.

Terminal Respiratory Secretions ("Death Rattle")

  • Why: near death, patients are too weak to swallow or clear oral and respiratory secretions. They pool, with a gurgling sound.
  • More distressing for the family than the patient, who is usually unconscious or minimally responsive.
  • The goal is not to eliminate every sound.
  • Glycopyrrolate (IV or tablet) and atropine (1% eye drops given sublingually): anticholinergics. They reduce new secretions, but do not remove those already there.
  • Also: gentle oral suction, oral care.
  • Family: "my loved one is drowning or suffocating." A chance for therapeutic communication and education.

Pathophysiology of the Dying Patient

SystemWhat HappensWhat You See
Cardiovascular
  • Myocardial failure: the cardiac output drops.
  • Blood shunting: vasoconstriction pulls blood from the periphery to protect the core vital organs.
  • Severe hypotension, then progressive bradycardia.
  • Mottling (livedo reticularis), starting in the lower extremities.
  • Cold, cyanotic skin. Weak or absent peripheral pulses.
Respiratory
  • Brainstem hypoxia: less perfusion to the medulla oblongata disrupts the automatic respiratory drive.
  • Terminal secretions: the swallowing reflex is lost, and secretions pool in the posterior pharynx.
  • Cheyne-Stokes breathing: periods of hyperpnea (driven by hypercapnia) alternating with apnea (driven by hypoxemia).
  • The death rattle.
Neuro
  • Cerebral hypoxia: less oxygen to the cerebral cortex.
  • Metabolic encephalopathy: toxins build up from liver and kidney failure.
  • An altered LOC: somnolence to unresponsiveness and coma.
  • Terminal delirium or agitation.
  • Hearing remains intact longest: keep speaking respectfully to the patient, even if unresponsive. Hearing works under low perfusion better than sight or touch.
Renal
  • AKI: severe hypotension drops the GFR to near zero.
  • Oliguria, progressing rapidly to anuria.
Hepatic
  • Metabolic arrest: the liver stops glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis.
  • Toxemia: waste such as ammonia is not cleared, speeding metabolic acidosis and systemic failure.
  • Profound hypoglycemia.
GI
  • Loss of peristalsis: blood is shunted away from the mesenteric bed.
  • Sphincter dysfunction: neuromuscular control is lost.
  • Functional anorexia, nausea, abdominal distension. Eating less is a normal part of dying.
  • Bowel and bladder incontinence.

Expected changes: many of these are the expected physiological changes as the organ systems fail.

Brain Death

What Brain Death Is

  • Complete, irreversible loss of brain and brainstem function.
  • Legally recognized as death.
  • Not a coma. Not a persistent vegetative state.
CriterionWhat It Means
Establish the cause
  • A known, irreversible cause of catastrophic brain injury and coma.
  • Brain death cannot be declared without one.
  • Examples: TBI, a massive intracranial hemorrhage, a prolonged anoxic brain injury.
Exclude reversible conditions
  • No sedatives, paralytics, severe metabolic abnormalities, drug intoxication, hypothermia or other confounding factors.
  • They suppress neurologic function and can make a patient appear brain dead.
Brainstem reflex exam
  • Fixed pupils. No corneal, gag or cough reflex: suctioned on the ventilator, does the patient cough?
  • No oculocephalic or caloric responses.
  • Absent reflexes suggest a loss of brainstem function.
Apnea test
  • Why: normally, rising CO2 makes the brainstem trigger a breath. One of the strongest stimuli to breathe.
  • How: disconnected from the ventilator under controlled conditions, watched in the room. The CO2 is allowed to rise.
  • No respiratory effort despite adequate CO2 stimulation supports brain death.
Ancillary testing, when needed
  • EEG.
  • Cerebral blood flow studies: is blood reaching the brain?

Organ Donation After Brain Death

The goals of care change: the patient is legally dead. No longer recovery, but preserving organ function so donation can occur, if the patient or family chose it.

SupportWhat to Do
Hemodynamic
  • Keep blood flowing to the organs: monitor the BP closely.
  • MAP at least 65 mm Hg, per protocol.
  • Give and titrate fluids and vasopressors as ordered.
Respiratory
  • Continue mechanical ventilation: still needed to deliver oxygen to the organs being preserved.
  • Monitor oxygenation and ABGs.
Diabetes insipidus
  • Loss of ADH is common after brain death: the pituitary may stop producing it.
  • Watch for: polyuria, hypernatremia, hypotension.
  • Treat: vasopressin.
Temperature
  • Maintain normothermia. The brain dead often lose temperature regulation and become hypothermic.
  • Warming devices as needed.
Family support
  • Clear explanations and compassion. Not making the decisions for them.
  • Encourage presence at the bedside.
  • Collaborate with KODA.

Diabetes insipidus is taught in the Neuro review

Caring for the Family

InterventionWhat It Looks Like
Assess their understanding
  • Of the illness and prognosis, first. Many providers have told them a lot, and they may not fully understand.
  • Know what they know before educating.
Prepare them for the withdrawal of life support
  • Explain what they may see and hear: irregular breathing, mottled skin, secretions. As when the patient is extubated.
  • Decreases fear of the unknown.
Assure them the patient will not suffer
  • The most common concern: will they be in pain?
  • Invite the family to say when they think the patient is in pain.
Assure them the patient will not be abandoned
  • When goals shift to comfort care: tell the patient and family who is responsible for the care.
  • Those people will be present and available when needed.
Emotional and spiritual support
  • Never impose your own beliefs. Families differ in beliefs, values and coping.
  • Connect them with resources meaningful to them: the chaplain, grief counselors, social workers.
Communication with the provider
  • Facilitate it.
Family presence
  • Many patients and families worry about dying alone. Encourage presence whenever possible.
  • Arrange chairs, provide privacy, encourage sitting at the bedside. Practical comfort items.

Emotional and Psychological Care

Meeting the Family's Emotional Needs

  • Assess individual needs without assumptions or judgment. What comforts one family may not help another.
  • Chosen family: partners, friends or others. A family may not look like what we traditionally expect.
  • Clear, consistent information about the prognosis and the dying process. Mixed messages and unfamiliar medical terms confuse: the team agrees on goals of care and prognosis.
  • Reinforce explanations and clarify misunderstandings. Families are overwhelmed, and hear things differently from different sources.
  • Support realistic expectations and patient or surrogate decision-making.
  • Preserve dignity: privacy, calm communication, compassionate presence.
  • A team effort: social work, chaplains, mental health, palliative care, hospice, bereavement services.

Communicating With Families

Family Conferences

  • Allow ample time for family members to express themselves.
  • Ask about the patient's wishes: "What has your loved one told you about their wishes?" "What would they have wanted?"
  • Aim for everyone to agree on the plan of treatment: providers, patient and family. Based on the patient's known or perceived preferences and values.
  • Why agree: fewer legal actions against providers, less patient and family anxiety, and the patient stays the focus.
  • Continuity of care: on a transfer, such as to a hospice or ventilator unit, pass on the history, prognosis, care needs, palliative interventions and psychosocial needs.
At the end of life, families need three things:
  • Information: accurate, about what is happening.
  • Support: as they make difficult decisions.
  • Reassurance: their loved one will stay comfortable and cared for.

Caring for Yourself

Caring for Yourself During End-of-Life Care

  • Acknowledge your emotions: grief, sadness, frustration, helplessness, questioning whether you did enough. Normal at every level of practice.
  • Remember your impact: success is not curing. It is relieving pain, dignity, supporting a family, a patient not dying alone.
  • Debrief and reflect: with a preceptor, mentor, manager or trusted colleague.
  • Maintain healthy boundaries: care deeply without carrying every patient's suffering. Not taking responsibility for what you cannot control.
  • Practice self-care: rest, exercise, relationships, hobbies, time away. Necessities, not luxuries.
  • Recognize compassion fatigue: emotional exhaustion, irritability, detachment from patients, feeling numb, loss of empathy. Catch it early.
  • Seek support when needed: faculty, mentors, counseling, employee assistance programs.
Presence can be just as meaningful as a procedure.
