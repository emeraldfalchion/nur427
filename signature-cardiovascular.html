The Cardiovascular Diagnoses

DiagnosisWhy They Are HereKey Cues and FindingsLabs and DiagnosticsPriority ProblemsHow It WorsensKey ActionsComplications in Other Systems
Acute coronary syndrome (ACS)
  • CAD: a plaque ruptures and a thrombus blocks a coronary artery.
  • Hypertension, hyperlipidemia, smoking, diabetes. Male, older, family history.
  • Chest pressure not relieved by rest, position or nitrates, lasting 20 minutes or more.
  • Nausea, diaphoresis, dyspnea, anxiety, dysrhythmias.
  • Atypical in women, older patients and patients with diabetes.
  • Troponin elevated in an MI, drawn serially.
  • 12-lead EKG within 10 minutes: ST elevation in a STEMI. ST depression or T wave inversion in an NSTEMI.
  • Potassium, magnesium, calcium.
  • Decreased cardiac tissue perfusion: the myocardium past the blockage gets no oxygen.
  • Impaired gas exchange when a second disease, such as COPD, lowers the oxygen supply.
  • Acute pain: a general concern.
  • More cells die: dysrhythmias, heart failure, cardiogenic shock.
  • Thromboembolism, rupture of part of the heart, pericarditis: more common after a true MI.
  • MONA-TAS: morphine, oxygen, nitroglycerin, aspirin, thrombolytics, anticoagulants, sedatives.
  • Reperfusion: a PCI within 90 minutes, thrombolytics within 30 for a STEMI, or a CABG.
  • Nitroglycerin: blood pressure first. Too low: morphine instead.
  • AKI after a cath: contrast dye is nephrotoxic. Watch the urine output, BUN and creatinine.
  • Lost cardiac output: the kidneys lose perfusion, and the work of breathing worsens.
Acute heart failure
  • New onset after a big MI or cardiac surgery.
  • Exacerbation of chronic HF. Example: a few days of really salty food.
  • Left-sided failure is related to hypertension and diabetes.
  • Both sides in the ICU. SOB the biggest, with fatigue. Crackles, blood-tinged sputum.
  • JVD, 3+ or 4+ pitting edema, ascites, hepatomegaly.
  • Low BP, dysrhythmias, extra heart sounds.
  • Echo: the ejection fraction, 40% or less in heart failure.
  • BUN and creatinine: low cardiac output starves the kidneys.
  • Potassium, watched with diuretics and digoxin.
  • Impaired gas exchange: the left ventricle cannot pump, and blood backs up into the lungs.
  • Excess fluid volume: the right side backs up into the venous circulation.
  • Decreased cardiac output: a floppy muscle cannot generate the blood pressure.
  • Cardiogenic shock: the BP falls and perfusion fails.
  • Dysrhythmias: less coronary flow, and the conducting cells die.
  • Keep the patient dry: IV furosemide or bumetanide, ultrafiltration.
  • Low BP: not fluid. Positive inotropes (milrinone, dobutamine) and vasopressors.
  • Sedation. An LVAD or VA ECMO. The four pillars, for life.
  • AKI: low cardiac output from heart failure is a pre-renal cause.
  • Ischemic bowel: heart failure and high-dose vasopressors cut the gut's blood flow.
  • Acute liver failure: heart failure exacerbating, an acute loss of perfusion.
Aortic aneurysm
  • Atherosclerosis weakens the aortic wall.
  • Male, older, smoking: the three huge risk factors.
  • In the ICU after a rupture or a repair.
  • Usually asymptomatic until it ruptures. Sometimes back or abdominal pain with a AAA.
  • Ruptured: very hypovolemic, chest pain, diaphoretic.
  • CT angiogram.
  • After a rupture or repair: BUN and creatinine, AST and ALT, bilirubin.
  • Deficient fluid volume: a rupture bleeds out.
  • Ineffective tissue perfusion: blood flow lost below the rupture.
  • Grows, then ruptures. A big rupture is usually fatal.
  • Avoid rupture: watched or repaired, by size. Smoking cessation.
  • After a repair: post-op care as after a CABG, with chest tubes.
  • Blood flow lost below the rupture: dead bowel, severe AKI, liver damage, pancreatic issues, poorly perfused legs.
Cardiac tamponade
  • Most common after a CABG or any open heart surgery. Also after a cath.
  • Chest trauma, a ruptured aorta, a ruptured ventricle after an MI. Pericarditis not watched closely.
  • Beck's triad: low BP, JVD, muffled heart sounds.
  • After surgery: chest tube output stops abruptly, with sudden hypotension and tachycardia.
  • H&H drop with no obvious bleeding, trended every 1 to 2 hours after cardiac surgery.
  • Decreased cardiac output: blood in the pericardial space keeps the heart from beating effectively.
  • Circulatory shock and cardiac arrest: a medical emergency.
  • Blocked chest tube: the surgeons may milk it.
  • After surgery: back to the OR. Otherwise: pericardiocentesis or a pericardial window.
  • None taught.
Pericarditis
  • Often after an MI or open heart surgery.
  • Chest trauma, infection, radiation, cancer, lupus.
  • Pericardial friction rub: the hallmark.
  • Very painful: mimics MI pain. Dyspnea, fatigue, anxiety.
  • ST elevation on the EKG. WBC and sed rate up.
  • Troponin, to rule out an MI. An echo (TTE).
  • Acute pain: a general concern.
  • Decreased cardiac output: it can cause a tamponade.
  • A tamponade, if not monitored closely.
  • Anti-inflammatories: ibuprofen, colchicine, ketorolac for a big one.
  • Rule out an MI. Listen for the rub.
  • None taught.
Valve disease and infective endocarditis
  • Aortic stenosis, mitral regurgitation: the two most common. Can be congenital.
  • Endocarditis: IV drug use, sepsis.
  • In the ICU after a valve replacement.
  • Valve disease: heart failure signs, exertional dyspnea, fatigue, chest pain, a murmur.
  • Endocarditis: a really sick infection. High temp, chills, diaphoresis, low BP, dysrhythmias.
  • Echo: the valves, the chambers and the ejection fraction.
  • Labs: none taught.
  • Decreased cardiac output: the blood backs up behind the valve.
  • Hyperthermia in endocarditis: bacteria in the bloodstream lodge on the valve as vegetations.
  • Heart failure: from the left side into the lungs, from the right into the periphery.
  • Endocarditis: the valve too damaged to save.
  • Valve replacement: post-op care as after a CABG.
  • Endocarditis: continuous IV antibiotics for 2 to 6 weeks. Prophylactic antibiotics for life.
  • None taught.
AV blocks
  • Age or ischemic disease, as in post-MI heart failure.
  • Bradycardia: the biggest concern.
  • Compromised cardiac output, more symptomatic with each degree.
  • The rhythm on the monitor or a 12-lead EKG.
  • Labs: none taught.
  • Decreased cardiac output: the rate is too slow.
  • Activity intolerance: not enough cardiac output brings fatigue.
  • 1st degree, 2nd degree type I, type II, then 3rd degree: complete heart block.
  • A pacemaker: no medication fixes a heart block.
  • Symptomatic bradycardia: a transvenous pacer.
  • Lost cardiac output: the kidneys lose perfusion, and the work of breathing worsens.

The Patient

Histories, Vital Signs and Medications

HPI: Daniel Brooks is a 63-year-old white man brought to the ED by his wife at 2130. At 2045, carrying groceries up the stairs, he felt a heavy pressure in the center of his chest. He sat down, but after 20 minutes of rest it had not eased, and his own nitroglycerin did nothing. He reports nausea, sweating, shortness of breath and feeling scared. He denies vomiting, fainting, palpitations, fever, or any change in his usual cough. For 2 years his chest tightness has come only with exertion and always eased with rest.

PMH: Hypertension, 12 years. Hyperlipidemia, 8 years. COPD (emphysema), diagnosed 6 years ago, no exacerbation in the past year. Stable angina, 2 years: chest tightness with exertion, relieved by rest.

SH: Married, lives with his wife in a two-story house. Retired machinist. Former smoker: a pack a day for 40 years, quit 3 years ago. Two or three beers on weekends. No drug use.

FH: Father died of an MI at 58. Mother has hypertension.

Admission VS: BP 158/94, HR 104 (sinus tachycardia with occasional PVCs), RR 24, SpO2 89% on room air, temperature 37.0 °C (98.6 °F), pain 8/10.

Home medications:

  • Amlodipine, for hypertension
  • Atorvastatin, for hyperlipidemia
  • Aspirin, an antiplatelet, for his CAD
  • Tiotropium, for COPD
  • Albuterol, for wheezing
  • Nitroglycerin, for chest pain

Allergies: Sulfa drugs (rash).

General Survey, Physical Exam, Labs and Diagnostics

  • General survey: 63-year-old man sitting upright in bed, a hand pressed to his chest. Pale and diaphoretic. Restless and anxious: "Am I having a heart attack?" Speaks in full sentences, pausing for breath. Clean and well groomed, in a hospital gown. His wife at the bedside. Alert and oriented × 4, GCS 15.
  • Pain: 8/10 pressure in the center of his chest, "like a heavy weight", constant since 2045, not relieved by rest or his own nitroglycerin.
  • Cardiovascular: S1 and S2 present, rapid, with occasional early beats. No murmur, gallop or rub. Telemetry: sinus tachycardia at 104 with occasional PVCs. No JVD at 45 degrees. No edema of the hands, legs, ankles or feet bilaterally. Radial and pedal pulses 2+ and equal bilaterally. Capillary refill under 3 seconds in fingers and toes. Skin cool and damp.
  • Respiratory: RR 24, slightly labored. Barrel chest. No use of the neck or thoracic accessory muscles. Breath sounds decreased in the posterior bases bilaterally, with expiratory wheezes in the posterior upper lobes. Anterior and lateral fields clear. Prolonged expiration. Infrequent, nonproductive cough.
  • Genitourinary: Foley placed in the ED, draining clear yellow urine at 50 mL/hr. No suprapubic tenderness or distention.
  • Labs: Troponin 0.84 ng/mL, elevated. BMP: sodium 139, potassium 3.6, chloride 102, CO2 27, BUN 16, creatinine 0.9, glucose 142.
  • Diagnostic: 12-lead EKG at 2135: sinus tachycardia at 104, ST depression and T wave inversion in two contiguous leads. No ST elevation.

Priority nursing problem

Supporting cues
  • Chest pressure 8/10 for over 45 minutes, not relieved by rest or nitroglycerin: ischemic pain, the hallmark of an MI.
  • Troponin 0.84 ng/mL, elevated: heart cells are dying.
  • ST depression and T wave inversion with no ST elevation: ischemia, read with the troponin as an NSTEMI.
  • HR 104 with occasional PVCs: a faster heart needs more oxygen, and ischemic conducting cells misfire.
  • SpO2 89% on room air: hypoxemia.
  • RR 24, slightly labored: tachypnea.
  • Breath sounds decreased in the posterior bases, expiratory wheezes, prolonged expiration, barrel chest: his COPD.
Relevance to the diagnosis
  • Years of hypertension, hyperlipidemia and 40 pack-years of smoking built atherosclerotic plaque in his coronary arteries: his stable angina. Tonight a plaque ruptured and a thrombus formed, partly blocking a coronary artery. The myocardium past it gets no oxygen or nutrients and is starting to die. His fast heart rate raises the demand while the supply is cut. Without reperfusion, the infarct grows.
  • Emphysema has damaged his alveoli, so less oxygen crosses into his blood, and that blood is all his ischemic myocardium gets. Hypoxemia severely impacts cardiac tissue and can set off rhythm changes, on top of the PVCs already seen. Oxygen improves the heart's supply. His target is an SpO2 of 88 to 92%, as for any patient with COPD.
Concern rating
  • 2, urgent. Every minute of ischemia costs heart muscle, so it must be treated quickly. His BP is holding, he is alert, and the rhythm is sinus: he is not expected to die within the hour.
  • 2, urgent. Low oxygen worsens the ischemia and can trigger dysrhythmias. He speaks in full sentences with no accessory muscles in use: not life-threatening within the hour.

The Story Goes On

Early the Next Morning

In the ED Mr. Brooks was given aspirin, to keep platelets from clumping on the clot, nitroglycerin, to open his coronary arteries, oxygen at 2 L/min by nasal cannula, and heparin, to keep the clot from growing. His pain eased to 2/10. He came to the CVICU at 2200, with a cardiac cath planned for the morning. At 0400 he presses the call light: the chest pressure is back at 7/10. More nitroglycerin does not touch it, and soon his BP is too low for any more. The monitor shows frequent PVCs, couplets, and a 6-beat run of VTach at 0425. He is pale, cool and sweating, and says, "I think I'm dying." His urine output has dropped to 20 mL/hr for the last 2 hours.

Updated VS: BP 96/60, HR 112 (sinus tachycardia with frequent PVCs), RR 26, SpO2 90% on 2 L/min by nasal cannula, temperature 37.1 °C (98.8 °F), pain 7/10.

Lab: Troponin 3.6 ng/mL, up from 0.84.

The Patient

Histories, Vital Signs and Medications

HPI: Loretta Hayes is a 74-year-old Black woman brought to the ED by her daughter at 1900. Over 4 days she has grown more and more short of breath, and today she is breathless at rest. She has slept sitting up in her recliner for 3 nights, because lying flat makes her gasp. Her legs are swollen, and her weight is up 3 kg since Sunday, after a weekend family reunion of ham, chips and canned soup. She reports fatigue, a cough with pink, blood-tinged sputum, nausea and no appetite. She denies chest pain, palpitations, fever and fainting. She has taken all of her medications.

PMH: Chronic heart failure since a large MI 4 years ago, with an ejection fraction of 30% a year ago. Hypertension, 20 years. Type 2 diabetes, 15 years. Chronic kidney disease, stage 3, baseline creatinine 1.6.

SH: Widowed, lives alone in a one-story house. Retired school cafeteria cook. Her daughter lives 10 minutes away. Never smoked. No alcohol or drug use.

FH: Mother had hypertension and died of a stroke at 70. Brother has type 2 diabetes.

Admission VS: BP 152/90, HR 112 (sinus tachycardia with frequent PVCs), RR 30, SpO2 85% on room air, temperature 36.8 °C (98.2 °F), pain 3/10. Weight 84 kg, up from 81 kg.

With fluid in the lungs, too little oxygen reaches her blood, and she breathes fast to make up for it.
