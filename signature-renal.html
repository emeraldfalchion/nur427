Renal

The Three Kinds of AKI

CauseWhy They Are HereKey Cues and FindingsLabs and DiagnosticsPriority ProblemsHow It WorsensKey ActionsComplications in Other Systems
Pre-renal
  • Hypoperfusion: a sudden, severe drop in BP (shock), or interrupted blood flow to the kidneys.
  • Very dehydrated, a lot of blood lost, low cardiac output from heart failure. Very common in septic shock.
  • The most common cause of AKI.
  • Hypotension, with tachycardia.
  • Hypotensive or hypovolemic for a while: the AKI may develop within the next day or two.
  • BUN: dehydrated, a false high. Read it with the creatinine.
  • Deficient fluid volume: too little volume to perfuse the kidneys.
  • Decreased cardiac output when heart failure is the cause.
  • Prolonged hypotension cuts kidney perfusion further, and can damage the kidneys more.
  • The oliguric phase begins about 1 day after a hypotensive event.
  • IV fluid replacement: crystalloid, normal saline or LR, to flush the kidneys, if the patient can tolerate it.
  • Vasopressors for hypoperfusion: tighter vessels, better perfusion to the kidneys.
  • Hypotension on IHD: fluid pulled off a patient already a little too dry, so the preload falls too low.
Intrarenal
  • Direct nephron injury: inflammation, toxins, drugs, infection or reduced blood supply.
  • Toxins and drugs are the big ones: the nephrotoxic antibiotics and contrast dye, high doses.
  • Reduced blood supply over time, from prolonged hypertension.
  • Most common: acute tubular necrosis (ATN).
  • Examples: glomerulonephritis from lupus or a virus, pyelonephritis, hypertensive glomerulonephropathy.
  • Hypertension for a short time, as in ATN, from RAAS malfunction.
  • A nephrotoxic drug or contrast dye in the history.
  • Peak and trough levels of a nephrotoxic drug.
  • Excess fluid volume: injured nephrons cannot excrete the fluid.
  • Electrolyte imbalance: a high potassium, no longer excreted.
  • More nephrotoxic exposure: poorly perfused kidneys asked to filter the ICU's antibiotics and contrast dye.
  • A nephrotoxic drug given in a fluid volume deficit: a double punch to the kidneys.
  • Address the drug: a team talk. Is it appropriate? Switch it, lower the dose, monitor peak and trough levels.
  • IV fluids flush out the nephrotoxic meds.
  • None taught.
Post-renal
  • Tubular obstruction: sudden obstruction of urine flow. Urine backs up from a problem past the kidneys.
  • An enlarged prostate, kidney stones, a bladder tumor, injury.
  • Not usually the cause in the critically ill, but can happen.
  • None taught.
  • None taught.
  • Impaired urinary elimination: urine cannot get past the blockage.
  • Electrolyte imbalance: a high potassium, no longer excreted.
  • None taught.

Every AKI, Whatever the Cause

  • Usually multifactorial: most often pre-renal plus intrarenal. Sepsis is the most common cause in the critically ill.
  • Oliguria: under about 0.5 mL/kg/hr, typically 30 mL/hr for an average adult.
  • Azotemia: the BUN and creatinine rise. Both up: the kidneys. The creatinine is the most reliable indicator.
  • Potassium builds up: the kidneys excrete it. A peaked T wave may be a potassium imbalance, so telemetry until it is back in range.
  • Metabolic acidosis from a lack of bicarb: the respirations change to compensate, and hyperventilation may be seen.
  • Uremia or azotemia: malaise, fatigue, disorientation, drowsiness. Super vague, but always look for them.

Every AKI: How It Worsens and What Is Done

  • The oliguric phase: fluid, potassium and wastes are retained, for 1 to 3 weeks.
  • Dialysis indications: acidosis, hyperkalemia, volume overload, uremia. A combination: low urine output alone is not one.
  • No medication fixes the kidneys: diuretics (furosemide, bumetanide) raise the urine output and flush potassium out. Monitor the electrolytes.
  • Renal replacement therapy when the rest is not working: IHD if hemodynamically stable, CRRT if fragile.
  • Other systems: volume overload brings crackles and pulmonary edema. Hyperkalemia brings dysrhythmias. Uremia changes the level of consciousness.
  • On IHD: hypotension, dysrhythmias from too much potassium pulled off, disequilibrium syndrome.
  • Afterward: the kidneys can recover. Some develop chronic renal disease.

The exemplar: a pre-renal plus intrarenal AKI, in the Acute Kidney Injury tab.

The Patient

Histories, Vital Signs and Medications

HPI: Rosa Delgado is a 69-year-old Hispanic woman brought to the ED by her son at 1400. For 4 days she has had vomiting and watery diarrhea, after a stomach bug went through her grandchildren, and she has kept almost nothing down. Two days ago she came to this ED with abdominal cramping, had a CT of the abdomen with contrast that showed nothing needing surgery, and was sent home with ondansetron. Since last night she has passed only a little dark urine. Today she is weak and dizzy when she stands, and her son found her drowsy and muddled about the day. She denies fever, blood in her stool or vomit, chest pain, shortness of breath, pain on urinating and flank pain.

PMH: Hypertension, 18 years. Chronic kidney disease, stage 3A, from her hypertension, diagnosed 3 years ago: baseline creatinine 1.3, eGFR 52. Hyperlipidemia, 10 years. Osteoarthritis of both knees.

SH: Widowed, lives with her son, his wife and their two young children. Retired seamstress. Never smoked. A glass of wine on holidays. No drug use.

FH: Mother had hypertension and kidney failure, on dialysis in her 70s. Father had type 2 diabetes.

Admission VS: BP 90/54 (MAP 66), HR 118 (sinus tachycardia), RR 24, deep and regular, SpO2 96% on room air, temperature 37.2 °C (99.0 °F), pain 4/10. Weight 62 kg, down from her usual 65 kg.

Home medications:

  • Amlodipine, for hypertension
  • Atorvastatin, for hyperlipidemia
  • Acetaminophen, for knee pain
  • Ondansetron, for nausea, since 2 days ago

Allergies: Codeine (vomiting).

General Survey, Physical Exam, Labs and Diagnostics

  • General survey: 69-year-old woman lying curled on her side on the stretcher, knees drawn up. Eyes open, but drifts off between questions. Speech slow and soft. Oriented to person and place, not to the date: GCS 14 (E4, V4, M6). Flat and tired: "I just feel so weak." Hair uncombed, lips dry and cracked, in a hospital gown after her soiled clothes were changed. Her son at the bedside.
  • Pain: 4/10 cramping across the lower abdomen, coming in waves before each loose stool. Last stool 1 hour ago, watery and brown, no blood.
  • Cardiovascular: S1 and S2 present, rapid and regular. No murmur, gallop or rub. Telemetry: sinus tachycardia at 118. Neck veins flat at 45 degrees. No edema of the hands, sacrum, legs, ankles or feet bilaterally. Radial and pedal pulses 1+, weak and thready, equal bilaterally. Capillary refill 4 seconds in fingers and toes. Skin cool and clammy. Mucous membranes dry and sticky.
  • Respiratory: RR 24, deep and regular. No use of the neck or thoracic accessory muscles. Breath sounds clear in the anterior, posterior and lateral fields bilaterally, with no crackles or wheezes. No cough. SpO2 96% on room air.
  • Genitourinary: Foley placed in the ED: 40 mL of dark, concentrated amber urine, then 10 mL/hr. No blood in the urine. Bladder not distended, no suprapubic tenderness, no flank tenderness bilaterally.
  • Labs: BMP: sodium 133, potassium 5.8 (high), chloride 104, CO2 16, BUN 74, creatinine 3.6 (baseline 1.3), glucose 98. ABG on room air: pH 7.30, PaCO2 30, HCO3 16, PaO2 88.
  • Diagnostic: 12-lead EKG at 1415: sinus tachycardia at 118, with tall, peaked T waves.

Priority nursing problem
  • Deficient fluid volume

Supporting cues
  • BP 90/54 with HR 118: hypotension with tachycardia, what a pre-renal AKI looks like.
  • Urine 10 mL/hr, dark and concentrated: oliguria, under her 0.5 mL/kg/hr, which at 62 kg is 31 mL/hr.
  • Radial and pedal pulses weak and thready, capillary refill 4 seconds, cool and clammy skin, flat neck veins, dry mucous membranes, 3 kg lost: little volume in her vessels.
  • Creatinine 3.6, up from her baseline of 1.3, with BUN 74: both up, so the kidneys. The BUN is higher still because dehydration gives it a false high.
  • Potassium 5.8: high.
  • Tall, peaked T waves on the 12-lead: a potassium imbalance, showing on the EKG.
  • pH 7.30, PaCO2 30, HCO3 16: a partially compensated metabolic acidosis. The low HCO3 is her kidneys failing to keep bicarb. The low PaCO2 is her lungs compensating: RR 24, deep, hyperventilation.
  • Stage 3A CKD: her kidneys already excrete less.
Relevance to the diagnosis
  • Four days of vomiting and diarrhea emptied her vessels, and her BP fell. The kidneys take 20 to 25% of cardiac output and are highly affected by changes in blood pressure: hypoperfusion, a pre-renal cause. Two days ago, while very dehydrated, she had contrast dye for her CT. Contrast dye is nephrotoxic, and a nephrotoxic drug in a fluid volume deficit is a double punch: direct nephron injury, an intrarenal cause, on top. Her kidneys had less to lose. Eighteen years of hypertension gave her CKD, and CKD carries a much higher risk for AKI. Rehydrated early, with the cause solved, an AKI can reverse.
  • The kidneys are primarily responsible for excreting potassium, and in CKD it is one of the first electrolytes to become abnormal. Making almost no urine, she cannot get rid of it, so it builds up. Her failing kidneys also cannot keep bicarb or excrete acid, so her blood has turned acidic, and in a metabolic acidosis H+ moves into the cells and K+ comes out, raising the potassium further. Hyperkalemia slows depolarization, and a hyperkalemic patient has a lot of rhythm issues. Above 6.0 come dysrhythmias that can be life-threatening.
Concern rating
  • 2, urgent. Every hour of low perfusion injures her kidneys further, and the volume must be replaced quickly. Her MAP of 66 is holding above 60, and she answers questions: she is not expected to die within the hour.
  • 2, urgent. The peaked T waves say the potassium must come down before a dysrhythmia comes. At 5.8, under 6.0, with a sinus rhythm, it is not life-threatening within the hour.

The Story Goes On

That Night

Mrs. Delgado came to the MICU at 1700. Her amlodipine was held, and by 2300 she had been given 3 L of normal saline, to rehydrate her and flush her kidneys. Her BP came up to 118/70 by 2100, but her urine output never rose. At 0300 she has made 6 mL/hr for 6 hours. She is more drowsy, opens her eyes only when spoken to, and knows her name but not where she is. She is scratching her arms and says she itches all over. Her grip is weak in both hands. Crackles have appeared in the posterior bases bilaterally, her neck veins are distended at 45 degrees, and she has 2+ pitting edema of both ankles and shins. She weighs 65.5 kg. The monitor shows tall, peaked T waves and new occasional PVCs. She needed 2 L of oxygen by nasal cannula at 0240, when her SpO2 fell to 91% on room air.

Updated VS: BP 164/92, HR 64 (sinus rhythm with occasional PVCs), RR 30, deep, SpO2 94% on 2 L/min by nasal cannula, temperature 36.9 °C (98.4 °F), pain 2/10. GCS 13.

Lab: BMP: sodium 130, potassium 6.6, CO2 12, BUN 96, creatinine 5.0.

A Complication in Another System

PartAnswer
ComplicationDisequilibrium syndrome (neurologic). The next morning, her third day, 2 hours into her second IHD run.
Why it could occurDisequilibrium syndrome is unique to hemodialysis, and most often comes in the first few treatments: the body is not used to so much fluid pulled off and put on so quickly. Yesterday's run pulled 2.4 L off and her BUN from 96 to 58 in 4 hours, and today's is pulling hard again.
Priority cuesDisoriented again, to place and time, after being oriented × 3 since yesterday's run: GCS 14. Restless and agitated, pulling at her blankets and reaching for the catheter in her neck. A new pounding headache, 7/10. Nauseated, and has vomited once. Her BP is 138/80 and HR 78, holding: this is not the hypotension that is IHD's biggest complication.
ActionsDependent: nephrology told at once, and per order the run is slowed, pulling less fluid off, or stopped for today. Independent: seizure precautions, since disequilibrium syndrome can bring seizures: suction set up and working, oxygen, and padded bed rails per policy. Her level of consciousness and orientation checked every 15 minutes, and someone with her to reorient her and keep her hands from the catheter, whose red and blue ports are for dialysis only.
EvaluationOrientation: back to oriented × 3 within an hour of slowing the run, with the headache easing. Worsening confusion, or a seizure, means the shift is still too fast.
