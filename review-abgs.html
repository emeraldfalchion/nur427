The Four Disorders

DisorderpHPrimary ChangeCompensation
Respiratory acidosis↓ Below 7.35PaCO2 ↑ above 45: carbonic acid excess. Hypoventilation retains CO2.HCO3 ↑, by the kidneys
Respiratory alkalosis↑ Above 7.45PaCO2 ↓ below 35: carbonic acid deficit. Hyperventilation blows off CO2.HCO3 ↓, by the kidneys
Metabolic acidosis↓ Below 7.35HCO3 ↓ below 24: bicarbonate deficit. Excess acids are added, or HCO3 is lost.PaCO2 ↓, by the lungs
Metabolic alkalosis↑ Above 7.45HCO3 ↑ above 29: too much HCO3, or not enough carbonic acid.PaCO2 ↑, by the lungs
ROME: Respiratory Opposite, Metabolic Equal.
  • Respiratory Opposite: pH ↑ and PaCO2 ↓ is alkalosis. pH ↓ and PaCO2 ↑ is acidosis.
  • Metabolic Equal: pH ↑ and HCO3 ↑ is alkalosis. pH ↓ and HCO3 ↓ is acidosis.

Common Causes

DisorderCommon Causes
Respiratory acidosis
  • Cardiopulmonary arrest.
  • Head injury.
  • Narcotics or sedatives: an overdose, or too much given.
  • Anesthesia, and after it while the drugs are metabolized.
  • Pulmonary disorders: acute asthma, COPD exacerbation, pneumonia, respiratory failure, atelectasis.
  • Pain: shallow breathing, no deep breaths because it hurts.
  • Abdominal distension.
  • Airway obstruction.
  • Chest wall deformities.
  • Neuromuscular problems: muscles too weak to ventilate, as in ALS or Guillain-Barré.
Respiratory alkalosis
  • Hyperventilation: acute anxiety, a panic attack, fear.
  • Increased metabolic demands: fever, infection, sepsis.
  • Medications that increase the breathing rate.
  • Hypoxia.
  • PE or lung disease.
  • CNS lesions: head injuries, brain lesions.
  • Ventilator settings: rate set too high, or tidal volume too big.
Metabolic acidosis
  • Renal failure: the kidneys cannot excrete H+.
  • Fistulas: HCO3 lost with the fluid and electrolytes.
  • Diabetes, type 1: DKA. Ketones are acids.
  • Lactic acidosis: lactate produced when the cells lack oxygen.
  • Prolonged diarrhea: the HCO3 released into the intestines to neutralize stomach acid is lost.
  • Starvation: body fat broken down, causing ketosis.
  • Medication overdose: aspirin.
  • Shock and cardiac arrest: lactic acid from tissues without enough oxygen.
Metabolic alkalosis
  • Too much base: excess baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) or antacids.
  • Prolonged vomiting: stomach acid (hydrochloric acid) is lost, and H+ with it.
  • NG tube: prolonged suctioning pulls hydrochloric acid out of the stomach.
  • Diuretics, especially loop diuretics: H+ lost in the urine.

Clinical Manifestations

DisorderClinical Manifestations
Respiratory acidosis
  • CNS: depression. Headache, restlessness, confusion, drowsiness, dizziness, disorientation. Decreased LOC, stupor, coma. Muscle weakness, hyperreflexia.
  • Breathing: hypoventilation, slow, or rapid and shallow. Dyspnea, respiratory distress: the patient cannot catch their breath. Hypoxia.
  • Cardiovascular: tachycardia, dysrhythmias. Decreased BP with vasodilation.
  • Potassium: hyperkalemia.
Respiratory alkalosis
  • CNS: over-excitability. Light-headedness, syncope. Confusion, blurred vision, lethargy. Paresthesias of the hands and feet, perioral. Hyperactive reflexes, seizures, coma.
  • Breathing: deep and rapid: tachypnea, hyperventilation. Once the patient passes out, breathing regulates and CO2 builds back up.
  • Cardiovascular: tachycardia. Decreased or normal BP.
  • Potassium: hypokalemia.
  • GI: nausea, vomiting.
Metabolic acidosis
  • CNS: lethargy, drowsiness, confusion, headache. Tremors, muscle cramps, muscle twitching. Paresthesias of the fingers or toes, perioral.
  • Breathing: Kussmaul respirations (DKA): the lungs' compensatory hyperventilation, blowing off CO2. Fruity odor breath (DKA).
  • Cardiovascular: hypotension. Warm, flushed skin from vasodilation.
  • Potassium: hyperkalemia: H+ moves into the cells and K+ comes out.
  • GI: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea.
Metabolic alkalosis
  • CNS: over-excitability. Restlessness followed by lethargy. Confusion, decreased LOC, dizziness, irritability. Tremors, muscle cramps. Paresthesias of the fingers and toes, perioral. Coma.
  • Breathing: respiratory depression: compensatory hypoventilation, holding on to CO2.
  • Cardiovascular: dysrhythmias, tachycardia.
  • Potassium: hypokalemia: H+ shifts out of the cells to buffer the alkalosis, and K+ moves in.
  • GI: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea.

Compensation

Compensation brings the pH back toward normal without correcting the underlying cause. The system not involved in the imbalance does it.

The Lungs: Respiratory Compensation

  • Compensate for: a metabolic problem.
  • How: hyperventilation blows off CO2 to correct an acidosis. Hypoventilation conserves CO2 to correct an alkalosis.
  • Speed: rapid, minutes to hours.
  • How long: cannot keep it up for long.

The Kidneys: Metabolic Compensation

  • Compensate for: a respiratory problem.
  • How: retain (reabsorb) HCO3 to correct an acidosis. Excrete HCO3 to correct an alkalosis. Also retain or excrete H+.
  • Speed: slow, hours to days.
  • How long: can keep it up for longer, if functioning properly.

Acute Respiratory Acidosis

  • pH low, PaCO2 high.
  • HCO3 normal: no time for the kidneys to compensate.
  • Examples: respiratory arrest.

Chronic Respiratory Acidosis

  • PaCO2 high: CO2 retained over time.
  • HCO3 high: the kidneys have had time to compensate. pH closer to normal, or normal when fully compensated.
  • Never a corrected ABG: the CO2 and the HCO3 stay high.
  • Breathing worsens, as with an infection: PaCO2 rises, HCO3 has not had time to follow, and the pH drops.
  • Examples: COPD.

Interpreting an ABG

StepWhat to Do
1. pH
  • Acid (below 7.35) or base (above 7.45)?
  • Normal, with PaCO2 or HCO3 abnormal: compensation has occurred. Decide which way it leans: 7.40 is neutral. Below 7.40 leans acid, above 7.40 leans base.
  • Examples: 7.36 and 7.38 lean acid, 7.45 leans base.
2. PaCO2
  • Acid (above 45), base (below 35) or normal?
  • Abnormal: a respiratory problem. Moves opposite to the pH: one up, one down.
3. HCO3
  • Acid (below 24), base (above 29) or normal?
  • Abnormal: a metabolic problem. Moves with the pH: both up or both down.
4. Compensation
  • Is the body restoring the pH by altering the buffer system not involved in the imbalance?
  • If so, that value moves in the same direction as the other component: both up or both down. One reads acid, the other base.
The pH points the way. The value that matches the pH, both acid or both base, names the problem. With a normal pH, match the way it leans.

The Four Levels of Compensation

Uncompensated

pH abnormal. PaCO2 or HCO3 abnormal, not both.

Partially Compensated

pH abnormal. PaCO2 and HCO3 abnormal: the body has started to pull the pH back.

Fully Compensated

pH normal. PaCO2 and HCO3 abnormal, but balanced. Neutralized, not corrected.

Corrected

pH normal. All values back to normal after the imbalance.
