Oxygen Therapy Principles

Principles of Oxygen Therapy

  • Needs an order, usually an admission order set to maintain SpO2. An emergency is the exception.
  • Start low and progress up as needed. A nasal cannula starts at 2 L, not 6. Use the lowest flow that maintains mental status and an acceptable SpO2.
  • Reported in percentages once past a nasal cannula at 6 L. Lowest 35%, highest 100%.
  • Humidification: used in all methods of O2 therapy, and required above 4 L/min. Monitor secretions to make sure it is adequate.
  • Inspired gas: about 37 degrees and 100% humidity, managed by respiratory therapy. Dry mucous membranes exchange gas less effectively.

Low Flow

  • Patient does the work to breathe. No pressure goes in.
  • Patient still pulls in some room air, so FiO2 is not as precisely controlled.
  • What most patients are on. An oxygen tent is low flow too.
  • More helpful for oxygenation.

High Flow

  • More precise and more controlled.
  • Better to supplement ventilation: more effective CO2 removal.
  • Stays constant when the breathing pattern is irregular, shallow or deep.
  • Typically needed when level of consciousness is significantly altered.

Oxygen Therapy Devices

High-Flow Nasal Cannula

  • Still a nasal cannula, but it gives positive pressure: it forces air into the airway.
  • 14 to 40 L/min, possibly higher, at about 60 to 100% O2.
  • Helps CO2 expulsion, so a great device for irregular breathing patterns.
  • Better than CPAP or BiPAP for most patients who are hypoxic or hypercapnic.
  • Patient has to be able to maintain head and chin position.
  • Respiratory therapy sets it up and manages its temperature and humidification.
  • Nurse: check it is plugged in and working, and report the liters and the percentage shown on the machine.

Other oxygen therapy devices are taught in the Respiratory review

Charted oxygen has to be correct. Check it at the wall at every assessment, usually every 4 hours. Oxygen comes unplugged.

Noninvasive Positive-Pressure Ventilation

Mode of ventilation to support gas exchange in a patient without an endotracheal tube (not intubated). Mask can be whole face or just nose.

CPAP

  • Continuous positive airway pressure.
  • Same air pressure on inhale and exhale.

BiPAP

  • An inspiratory positive airway pressure (IPAP) and an expiratory positive airway pressure (EPAP).
  • Different pressures for inhalation and exhalation.

When It Is Used, and Its Limits

  • For many respiratory conditions, usually severe. First line to prevent intubation, or used right at extubation.
  • Very helpful for getting CO2 off. Not as helpful in oxygenation respiratory failure.
  • Patient has to be able to maintain their own airway.
  • Not a long-term solution, though it can run continuously in the hospital: no eating or drinking with the mask on, and a lot of aspiration risk.
  • Respiratory therapy coordinates and controls the modes, usually based on pressure control or PEEP.
  • Examples: after a procedure, or while too much fentanyl wears off.

CPAP and BiPAP are taught in the Respiratory review

Airway Management

Airway is the most important aspect of any oxygenation or ventilation management. The patient has to be able to maintain it, and it starts with positioning.
MethodWhat to Know
Positioning
  • Head tilt-chin lift or jaw thrust. Comes first: airway open and able to take in air.
  • Not always enough with an altered level of consciousness or weakness.
Oral airway
(oropharyngeal airway)
  • Keeps the tongue from falling back and obstructing the pharynx.
  • For acute depression of level of consciousness. Short term only.
  • Often used in CPR until the patient can be intubated, with bag-mask ventilation over the top of it.
  • Correct size needed. Too short does not work: it has to get up and around the back of the tongue.
  • Also helps with oral suctioning.
Nasopharyngeal airway
(nasal trumpet)
  • Used to suction through: a red rubber or suction catheter goes down it when the cough is weak or secretions are hard to expel.
  • Second choice when an oral airway will not work: the patient is seizing or the jaw is too tight.
  • Better tolerated than an oral airway.

Artificial Airways

Endotracheal Tube

Endotracheal Tube (ETT or ET Tube)

  • The most common way to secure an airway. Inserted through the mouth into the trachea. Nose to trachea is possible with oral trauma, but not common.
  • Time to intubate when CPAP, BiPAP or an oral airway does not give the support needed. A big indication: a lot of secretions that are hard to contain.
  • Sized #6 to #9. Most common: 7 to 7.5 for a female, 7.5 to 8 for a male.
  • Can usually be suctioned through if placed in the hospital, with a red rubber catheter or inline suctioning.

Preparing to Intubate: The Nurse's Responsibilities

  • A good working IV.
  • Anticipate IV sedation and analgesia, short term or long term. Being intubated is not comfortable.
  • Patient is typically paralyzed momentarily with rapid sequence intubation (RSI) drugs.
Part or MarkingWhat to Know
Radiopaque line
  • Shows on the chest x-ray that verifies where the tube is.
Cuff
  • At the end of the tube. Inflated once the tube is known to be in the trachea, not the esophagus.
  • Seals the space between the tube and the trachea, and holds the tube in place.
  • Cuff inflated: the patient cannot speak.
  • Pressure too low: the tube may move. Too great: damage to the surrounding tissue.
Pilot balloon
  • How the cuff is inflated and deflated. Air in it means the cuff is inflated.
  • Nurse checks it at every assessment. Inflating, deflating and moving the tube belong to respiratory therapy.
Marking at the lip
  • Where the tube sits, in centimeters: "26 at the lip".
  • Check it all the time.

Confirming Placement After Intubation

  1. Bilateral breath sounds, listened for by whoever intubated, often anesthesia or the ED.
  2. CO2 sensed by a marker hooked up to the ET tube.
  3. Chest x-ray ASAP.

Care of the Intubated Patient

CheckWhat to Know
Verify placement,
every assessment
  • Same side of the mouth, same size, same centimeter marking at the lip.
  • Chest x-rays are repeated routinely. The nurse does not interpret them, but reads the report that the tube is where it should be.
Oral care
  • Patient cannot do it. Every 2 hours with an oral care kit: wash out the mouth and clean the tube.
  • Prevents ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP). Patients on a ventilator have a much higher risk of pneumonia.
Verify secure
  • Same place, held by a securement device, not tape on the face: fewer ulcerations.
  • A patient fighting the tube can tongue it out, even unconsciously.
Cuff inflated
  • Pilot balloon is inflated.
Tube clear
  • Nothing obstructing it. Patients still cough through the tube, and secretions sit at mouth level: suction them out.
  • Humidity inside the tube is not a concern.
Not bitten
  • Biting the tube is a big deal. Devices can be put in to prevent it.
  • Often why sedation is needed, when the patient cannot follow directions.
Sides of the mouth switched
  • Usually done by respiratory therapy. The nurse monitors that it is done.
  • A tube left sitting on the tongue or in the mouth causes ulcerations.
Secure to the ventilator
  • All connectors secure. They pop apart, and patients and family members pull them apart.
Skin intact
  • In the mouth and at the tape or securement device. Pressure ulcers are common here.

Endotracheal intubation is not a long-term solution: it invites pressure ulcers, VAP and injury to the trachea. A longer-term need means a transition to a tracheostomy.

Tracheostomy

TopicWhat to Know
Why it is placed
  • Preferred airway for a patient requiring a long-term airway.
  • Vent dependence: the patient cannot get off the ventilator. A patient with a trach may or may not be ventilated.
  • Weakness and a long-term inability to clear secretions, such as needing suction every 2 hours.
Cuff
  • Inflated when the patient is on the ventilator, as the ET tube cuff is. All the air given goes in, and exhaled CO2 does not go around the trach.
Care
  • Oral care and trach care. Skin integrity around the trach is crucial.
Suctioning
  • The connection to the ventilator can have its own suction port, as with an ET tube.
  • Otherwise a red rubber catheter, which is a little gentler on the airway, or a clear plastic one.

The two types of trach, the nurse's job, the cuff, suctioning and what to keep at the bedside are taught in the Respiratory review

Mechanical Ventilation

Purpose of Mechanical Ventilation

  • Support the respiratory system.
  • Respiratory failure: the patient cannot clear their own secretions or cannot perform adequate gas exchange.
  • Minimizing oxygen demand: the patient is sedated and paralyzed, as in sepsis and trauma, even without a major respiratory problem. Sedation and paralysis lower level of consciousness, so the respiratory system has to be supported.
  • Positive pressure ventilation: air is forced into the lungs, not passively accepted, even when the patient fights it.

Six Things the Nurse Is Responsible for Knowing

SettingWhat to Know
Mode
  • Synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation (SIMV).
  • Pressure regulated volume control (PRVC).
  • Pressure controlled (PC).
  • Volume controlled (VC).
  • Pressure support (PS): the weaning mode.
  • Airway pressure release ventilation (APRV).
FiO2
  • Percent of oxygen the patient gets.
  • 35 to 100%. Usually 40% at the lowest, and changed in increments of 10.
Tidal volume
  • How much air goes into the lungs with each breath.
  • Set by the ventilator in some modes and controlled by the patient in others. Monitored closely.
  • Common setting: 6 to 10 mL/kg.
Inspiratory pressure
  • How much force the ventilator needs to push air in. Tells about lung compliance and how sick the lungs are.
  • Can be set, depending on the mode, but usually is not.
  • Peak inspiratory pressure is watched, not set. 25 is pretty normal.
  • Common pressure control setting: 5 to 25.
Rate
  • Respiratory rate. Controls CO2 levels.
  • Set by the ventilator or by the patient, depending on the mode. A set rate is the minimum: the patient can breathe more often.
  • Common setting: 10 to 25, raised for acidosis.
PEEP
  • Positive end-expiratory pressure: a little extra pressure at the end of expiration that pops the alveoli open, for alveoli that are collapsed or not working well.
  • Promotes oxygenation and gas exchange, and helps more than tidal volume does.
  • Can be very dangerous: it forces the alveoli open and can cause a lot of trauma to the lungs. Tidal volume can be damaging too.
  • Kept at the lowest level possible.
  • Common setting: 5 to 20.

Complications of Mechanical Ventilation

ComplicationWhat to Know
ET tube out of position
  • Securement issue: the patient tongues the tube out, or it moves during transport.
  • Monitored with chest x-rays and the ventilator settings.
  • Tidal volumes and pressures changing for no clear reason: check whether the tube has moved and whether the cuff is inflated.
Unplanned extubation
  • Most of the time an emergency. Sometimes it means the patient no longer needs to be intubated.
  • Monitor oxygenation and think about positioning. Bag-mask ventilation at 100% is probably needed, depending on level of consciousness. A code may need to be called.
  • The nurse makes that decision. A patient who was on pressure support is probably doing okay.
Laryngeal and tracheal injury
  • Tube sits along the lining of the trachea. Tube movement (a thrashing head, the tube sliding up and down) and excess cuff pressure cause abrasions.
  • Respiratory team monitors cuff pressures.
Oral mucosa damage
  • Oral care, rotating the tube, monitoring the skin. Moisturizer for the mouth helps.

Pulmonary Issues Related to Mechanical Ventilation

IssueWhat to Know
Barotrauma
  • Pressure trauma: injury to the lungs from ventilation. Alveoli are injured or ruptured by excessive pressure and excessive inflation.
  • Seen a lot in ARDS, where damaged alveoli need a lot of PEEP. Too high, too hard or too long causes it.
  • Air escapes: subcutaneous emphysema, pneumothorax.
  • Why PEEP is used carefully and tidal volumes are kept appropriate for the patient.
Oxygen toxicity
  • Too much oxygen leads to pathological changes. How bad depends on how long.
  • Titrate the O2 down as quickly as possible, within safe margins, monitored by PaO2 on an ABG or by SpO2.
  • Can mimic ARDS. Not seen much.
Respiratory acidosis or alkalosis
  • Ventilator controls gas exchange, so it can blow off too much CO2 or retain too much.
  • ABGs, usually daily, for a ventilated patient, whether by ET tube or trach.
Infection
  • VAP is the biggest concern, and the focus is prevention.
  • VAP bundle: managing oral secretions, good oral care, a cuff inflated appropriately, effective suctioning, a quicker transition to a trach.
Dysphagia or aspiration
  • Intubated more than 24 hours: high risk of swallowing difficulty and aspiration after extubation.
  • The throat and the gag take a while to readjust.

Weaning Considerations

Readiness and Weaning Trials

  • Approached in a systematic fashion, always thinking about readiness: can the patient breathe on their own?
  • Weaning trial: a pressure support trial, which makes the patient do the work. Done every day.
  • Not appropriate for a patient who is sedated and paralyzed.
  • Tried for a few minutes first, then for longer. Sometimes 15 to 30 minutes is enough.
  • Ready: awake, following instructions, squeezing hands, understanding what is going on. Assess the cough and how weak the patient is too.

Extubation

  • Emergency supplies ready.
  • Positioning: sitting as high as possible, head of bed at 90 degrees.
  • Calm environment, usually without family at the bedside.
  • Lights on.
  • Pain managed, but level of consciousness not depressed.
Nothing to drink until a bedside swallow is done, even after intubation for a procedure only. The patient will be thirsty. Start with ice chips and advance slowly to water as tolerated. Coughing: go back a step, and a speech evaluation may be needed.
