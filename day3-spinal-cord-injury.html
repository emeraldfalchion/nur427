Spinal Cord Injury

Spinal Cord Injury (SCI)

Who Gets SCI, and How

  • Most common at ages 16 to 30.
  • Causes: motor vehicle accidents, falls, acts of violence (gunshots, stabbings), sports injuries, diving accidents.
  • TBI and SCI often go hand in hand.
  • The level of injury decides the long-term effects to anticipate. C4 and C6 injuries: tetraplegia. T6 and L1 injuries: paraplegia.

Pathophysiology of SCI

What Happens to the Cord

  • With or without injury to the vertebrae, which house the spinal cord.
  • Complex, multifaceted biochemical changes in the cord: the secondary injury.
  • As in TBI and stroke, the biochemical inflammatory response on top of the primary injury causes much of the damage.

Spinal Cord Edema

From the inflammatory response. Compresses the tissue and the blood vessels. Can ascend or descend from the level of injury.

Microscopic Hemorrhages

Vascular changes: bleeding into the gray and white matter. The edema worsens.

Ischemia

Blood flow decreases further.

Axonal Degeneration

The nerves fail to conduct: the permanent loss of function.

Secondary injury in TBI is taught in Brain Injury and Stroke

Two Types of Shock

Spinal Shock

  • Electrical silence of the cord below the level of injury: complete loss of motor, sensory and reflex activity, at the level of injury and below.
  • Begins within minutes of the injury. Can last up to 6 weeks.
  • The permanence of the injury is not known until it resolves: the secondary injury can still spread up or down.
  • Resolution: usually signaled by the return of deep tendon reflexes. Rarely, motor and sensory function return.

Neurogenic Shock

  • Disruption of autonomic function below the level of injury: the pathways are damaged.
  • Sympathetic input lost: vasodilation, a distributive shock.
  • Manifests as hypotension, bradycardia, hypothermia.
  • Can occur during spinal shock. Duration variable.
  • Resolution: signaled by the return of sympathetic tone.
  • Not always: depends on whether the lesion is complete or incomplete. More likely with an injury at T6 or higher.

Distributive shock is taught in Shock, Sepsis, MODS

Complete vs Incomplete Lesion

LesionFunction Below the Level of Injury
CompleteTotal, permanent loss of sensory and motor function.
IncompleteSome motor and sensory function spared.

The lesion type reflects the severity of the injury.

Assessment of SCI

Airway and Respiratory: The First Priority

Why Breathing Is at Risk

  • An injury at the cervical or thoracic level can affect the diaphragm.
  • No nerve stimulation to the diaphragm: no independent breathing.
  • Paralysis of the diaphragm: intubation and mechanical ventilation.

C1 to C3

Ventilator dependent.

C4 or C5

Will need a phrenic nerve pacemaker.

C5 to C6

Typically intact diaphragmatic breathing. Varying impairment of the intercostal and abdominal muscles.

T6 and above: very high risk for breathing issues. An incomplete lesion above T6 varies: watch these patients very closely for airway and respiratory problems.

Assessing the Other Systems

AssessmentWhat to Know
Neurologic
  • Sensory, motor and reflex function.
Temperature management
  • T6 and higher: unable to regulate body temperature.
  • Febrile or hypothermic: more likely to have prolonged issues.
  • Monitor closely and continuously, especially in the first several days.
  • Example: a continuous bladder temperature.
Hemodynamics
  • At risk from neurogenic shock, especially T6 and higher.
  • A significant SCI: in the ICU at least 7 days after the injury, for early detection and management of an unstable heart rate and BP.
  • Likely bradycardic: from hypothermia, hypoxia or neurogenic shock.
Bowel and bladder function
  • Function lost: spinal shock leads to atony of the bowel and bladder.
  • High risk for urinary retention.
  • High risk for paralytic ileus.
Skin
  • High risk of pressure ulcers and skin deterioration: motor and sensory function lost, impaired circulation, immobility.
Psychosocial
  • A life-changing, unexpected, horrible event: much psychosocial and psychological support needed.
  • Reach out to get these patients the support they need, and provide it yourself as the bedside nurse.

Interventions for SCI

Anytime the brain or nerves are involved: maintain a patent airway and respiratory function.
InterventionWhat to Know
Skin assessment
  • Thorough skin assessments, for the pressure ulcer risk above.
Immobilization
  • Usually immobilized until cleared by the neurosurgery team.
  • Unstable vertebral fracture: surgery, usually within 24 hours.
  • Stable fracture: may need a halo vest, so the patient is not moving and further damaging the spinal cord.
Perfusion
  • Goal MAP 85 to 90, for about 7 days: a higher goal, for spinal cord perfusion.
  • Minimize systolic hypotension during this time: it can cause further damage and secondary injury.
Bowel and bladder regimen
  • Often an NG tube and abdominal assessments.
  • Bladder: often long-term catheters, or a bladder program.
  • Bowel: maintain bowel programs, with enemas.
Heparin and VTE prophylaxis
  • Venous stasis: limbs not moving, so a very high risk for DVT and PE.
Nutrition
  • A hypermetabolic state: prompt nutritional support. Very important, as in TBI.

Preventing DVTs and PEs is taught in the Respiratory Diseases review

Autonomic Dysreflexia

Autonomic DysreflexiaWhat to Know
What it is
  • A chronic complication of SCI.
  • An exaggerated response from the sympathetic nervous system (SNS): the SNS is completely activated, and the parasympathetic too.
  • The body's protective response.
Who and when
  • An injury at T6 or above.
  • Typically after the initial spinal shock resolves.
Triggers
  • Usually a problem the patient cannot sense.
  • Bladder: a kinked catheter, a very distended bladder.
  • Bowel: fecal impaction.
  • Skin: clothes too tight, wrinkled bed sheets.
  • Temperature not controlled adequately.
Signs and symptoms
  • Pounding headache.
  • Bradycardia.
  • Elevated, uncontrolled BP.
  • Diaphoresis and flushing above the level of injury.
  • High levels of anxiety.
Treatment
  • Fix whatever is causing it, and it can resolve.
  • Supportive care.
