The Kidneys in Critical Illness

The kidneys are the primary regulator of the body's internal environment: fluid and electrolyte balance, and metabolic waste and toxin elimination. The excreter.

Why Kidney Injury Is So Common in the Critically Ill

  • They take 20 to 25% of cardiac output: highly affected by changes in cardiac output and blood pressure. Tons of tiny vessels respond to them.
  • They filter the ICU's nephrotoxic meds: antibiotics, contrast dye, high doses. Poorly perfused, and asked to work harder.
  • Dehydration: as a main driver of fluid and electrolyte balance, they are highly affected by fluid balance.

What Fails When the Kidneys Fail

FunctionNormallyWhen the Kidneys Fail
PotassiumPrimarily responsible for excreting potassium.Potassium builds up. Be very careful with it.
Blood pressure regulationA blood pressure regulator: home of the RAAS (renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system).Alterations in blood pressure regulation.
RBC productionSecrete erythropoietin.No erythropoietin: anemia. A longer-term effect.

Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)

A sudden decline in kidney function that causes fluid, electrolyte and acid-base imbalances.

Primary FeatureWhat It Is
AzotemiaThe buildup of nitrogenous waste products. Seen in the BUN and creatinine.
OliguriaLack of urine output. Normal output is about 0.5 mL/kg/hr: typically 30 mL/hr for an average person, down to 25 for a really small person.

Causes

Usually multifactorial: most often hypoperfusion plus nephron injury, so pre-renal plus intrarenal. Sepsis is the most common cause in the critically ill. Knowing the likely cause shows how to correct it.
CauseWhat to Know
Pre-renal
  • Hypoperfusion: a sudden and severe drop in blood pressure (shock), or interrupted blood flow to the kidneys from severe injury or illness.
  • Very common in septic shock.
  • Examples: very dehydrated, a lot of blood lost, low cardiac output from heart failure.
Intrarenal
  • Direct nephron injury: direct damage to the kidneys by inflammation, toxins, drugs, infection or reduced blood supply.
  • Toxins and drugs are the big ones: the nephrotoxic meds.
  • Reduced blood supply over time, from prolonged hypertension. Not sudden, severe hypoperfusion.
  • Examples: glomerulonephritis from lupus or a virus, pyelonephritis, hypertensive glomerulonephropathy.
Post-renal
  • Tubular obstruction: sudden obstruction of urine flow.
  • Not usually the cause in the critically ill, but can happen.
  • Examples: an enlarged prostate, kidney stones, a bladder tumor, injury.

The three kinds of cause, and the most common, are taught in the Renal review

Onset and Prognosis

Onset

  • Rapid: hours to days.
  • Several hours of really poor perfusion and the kidneys take an immediate hit: no urine output, and a bump in BUN and creatinine.
  • That is an AKI, even when it needs less intervention and never becomes months of care or CKD.
  • Examples: a lot of blood lost in surgery or trauma, acute heart failure.

Prognosis

  • Reversible. Rehydrated and the problem solved, the kidneys can be repaired.
  • Keys: early identification and treatment.

Signals to Monitor Closely and Notify

  • A risk: a lot of blood lost in the OR, a big long surgery, a very nephrotoxic antibiotic, or several procedures with contrast dye while very dehydrated.
  • And low urine output for several hours.
  • Then: monitor the urine output and the BUN and creatinine closely, and mention them to the team.

Assessment

AssessWhat to Know
Blood pressure
  • Changes are common in AKI. Monitor closely.
  • Pre-renal: watch closely for hypotension, with tachycardia.
  • Intrarenal, such as acute tubular necrosis: hypertension for a short time, from RAAS malfunction.
Respirations
  • May change to compensate for the acid-base imbalance. The respiratory system responds very quickly.
  • Severe AKI, very acidotic from a lack of bicarb: breathing off CO2, so hyperventilation may be seen.
Uremia or azotemia
  • Signs and symptoms: malaise, fatigue, disorientation, drowsiness.
  • Super vague: the same as the low perfusion and sepsis that put patients at risk. Hard to pin on AKI unless the BUN and creatinine are through the roof with nothing else going on.
  • Always look for them.

AKI Interventions

No medication fixes the kidneys. Care is supportive: flush the system and improve cardiac output, so the kidneys can reverse and repair themselves.
InterventionWhat to Know
Address the cause
  • Meds contributing? A team talk: is the drug appropriate? Switch it, lower the dose, monitor peak and trough levels?
  • Hypoperfusion? Vasopressors to tighten the vessels, for better perfusion and blood pressure to the kidneys. Hydrate.
Diuretics
  • Furosemide, bumetanide: increase the urine output.
  • Help with the buildup of electrolytes: flush the potassium out.
IV fluid replacement
  • Flush the kidneys: a lot of fluid and perfusion, to clear the filter. Many times, if the patient can tolerate it.
  • Crystalloid: isotonic saline (normal saline) or LR.
  • Pre-renal causes mainly. Helps intrarenal causes too: flushes out the nephrotoxic meds.
Dietary management
  • Longer-term cases: the dietary considerations for kidney problems.
Electrolytes
  • Monitor them.

Renal Replacement Therapy

The primary treatment for AKI: dialysis. Used when the other interventions are not working, or the AKI is getting more severe.

IndicationLook For
AcidosisAn acid-base imbalance, typically acidotic, that needs correcting.
HyperkalemiaThe electrolytes on the labs.
Volume overloadCrackles, pulmonary edema, 2+ to 4+ pitting edema in the lower extremities.
UremiaRising BUN and creatinine. Level of consciousness changes that cannot be put down to anything else.
Low urine output alone is not an indication. The indications are a combination of things.

How Dialysis Works

  • Removes toxins and fluid through a semipermeable membrane. Works toward equilibrium, pulling solutes out of the blood: what the kidneys should be doing.
  • Corrects uremia, azotemia and acidosis, and pulls fluid off.
  • Settings: the rates of infusion, how much it pulls off, and what it pulls off. Managed by the renal or nephrology team.
  • The nurse monitors the settings, with a basic understanding of what is going on.
  • Monitor the indication for improvement, such as hyperkalemia and volume overload. BUN and creatinine should improve. Urine output may or may not.

Vascular Access

Temporary

Most common for AKI. A trialysis port, in the internal jugular.

Semi-Permanent

Tunneled catheters. The subclavian is kept for long-term or semi-permanent access.

Permanent

AV fistula.

A trialysis port is for dialysis only. The red and blue ports connect to the dialysis machine and nothing else. The purple port takes other access only occasionally, with the team's approval.

Intermittent or Continuous Dialysis

Intermittent Hemodialysis (IHD)

  • Patient: hemodynamically stable, typically on no drips for blood pressure. Can even be ventilated, with a lot going on.
  • Pace: 3 to 4 hours at a time. Intense and harsh on the body.
  • Where: the dialysis unit, or sometimes the bedside.

Continuous Renal Replacement Therapy (CRRT)

  • Patient: fragile, such as with sepsis, a very unstable blood pressure, or a lot of blood lost. Often chosen for AKI for this reason.
  • Pace: continuous. Much more gentle: the rate can be slowed down or sped up, with much more nuanced control.
  • Where: the ICU only.
  • Provides: ultrafiltration and diffusion, continuously. Does the work of the kidneys while the body heals.
  • Indications: the same as IHD.

Is the Patient a Candidate for IHD?

  • Blood pressure stable with no IV meds? Chronic PO meds do not count.
  • Oxygen pretty stable on whatever device?
  • Tolerating the usual things?
  • If not: probably not a candidate for IHD.

Types of CRRT

  • Slow continuous ultrafiltration (SCUF)
  • Continuous venovenous hemofiltration (CVVH)
  • Continuous venovenous hemodialysis (CVVHD)
  • Continuous venovenous hemodiafiltration (CVVHDF)

IHD Complications

ComplicationWhat to Know
Hypotension
  • The biggest and most concerning. Very common.
  • Cause: usually pre-existing intravascular hypovolemia. Fluid is pulled off the vascular system of a patient already a little too dry, and the preload falls too low.
  • Prolonged hypotension cuts kidney perfusion further, and can damage the kidneys more.
Dysrhythmias
  • Cause: usually potassium imbalances. Commonly, too much potassium pulled off: hypokalemia.
  • Replacing potassium with baseline kidney impairment is a delicate balance.
Muscle cramping
  • Cause: ischemia to the skeletal muscles from aggressive fluid removal, or electrolyte imbalances.
  • More related to chronic renal failure.
Decreased PaO2
  • Usually not an issue unless critically ill.
  • A ventilated patient not doing well with oxygenation may not be a good candidate for IHD.
Infection
  • Cause: the big temporary dialysis port attracts bacteria.

Disequilibrium syndrome and the other complications of hemodialysis are taught in the Renal review

IHD Nursing Responsibilities

Caring for a Patient on IHD

  • Reassess the patient: tolerating IHD on Monday does not mean Wednesday. Fragility can come on very quickly: vitals, labs and oxygen change as the cause worsens or the meds change.
  • Communicate with the team if the patient may not tolerate it today. They can pull less fluid off, or go slower.
  • Labs before and after dialysis: often in the order, but clarify.
  • No water-soluble meds before dialysis: often antihypertensives and sedation meds.
  • An antihypertensive before dialysis: the blood pressure drops, then dialysis pulls more fluid off.

Drugs dialyzed out, and drugs that build up to toxic levels, are taught in the Renal review

CRRT Nursing Responsibilities

ResponsibilityWhat to Know
Know the parameters
  • The programmed parameters, the dialysate and the solutions match the orders.
  • Know the mode, and what it is doing to the patient.
Intake and output
  • The most important: extremely careful, usually hourly.
  • Each hour: fluid pulled off by CRRT, urine output, IV fluids given.
  • A lot goes in: usually continuous IV fluids and continuous tube feeding through a Dobhoff tube, maybe free water.
  • Chart it closely: is enough coming off? Fluid removal is one of the big things CRRT does.
Hemodynamic monitoring
  • An arterial line in the vast majority, for close blood pressure monitoring.
  • Patient declining: slow the fluid removal, or the CRRT rate.
  • Stable enough to need no arterial line: probably ready to move to IHD soon.
Temperature
  • The blood comes back cold: it leaves the body through an external machine. The machines have heaters, but patients are often internally cold.
  • Often need a Bair Hugger, and usually hourly temps.
Filter clotting
  • Assess the filter for clotting. A normal filter is all red blood. A clot shows first at the top. A clotted filter means that machine is done.
  • Usually on a blood thinner: enoxaparin, maybe a heparin drip. Not a requirement, but consistent: immobile patients at higher risk for DVT.
Labs
  • Very close: BUN and creatinine and an electrolyte panel, usually every 8 hours, to keep a nice equilibrium.

Long-Term Outlook

After an AKI

  • Kidneys can recover: CRRT, or even a while on IHD, does not mean lifelong dialysis. Corrected quickly enough, the kidneys can be saved.
  • Some develop chronic renal disease: lifelong IHD, or a kidney transplant.

AKI on Top of CKD

  • Much higher risk for a further AKI with CKD or end-stage renal disease.
  • Shows as a bump in BUN and creatinine from baseline. May need CRRT for a time.
  • Harder to spot: urine output may be lower than healthy kidneys make, or none at all on dialysis at home.
  • Monitor closely: BUN and creatinine, electrolytes, symptoms.
