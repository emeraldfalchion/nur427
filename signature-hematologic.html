The Hematologic Diagnoses

DIC
  • Always set off by another condition: vessel injury from a disease state, tissue injury, or a foreign body in the bloodstream.
  • Trauma, burns and sepsis are the most common triggers. Infections and inflammatory states disrupt coagulation.
  • Clots and bleeds at once: microemboli in the small vessels, and any area that can bleed will bleed.
  • Bleeding and oozing from insertion sites.
  • Organ ischemia: skin, lungs and kidneys most often. Skin signs sometimes only on the hands, the feet and the tips of the toes.
  • Very painful: organs and tissues are dying.
  • Diagnosed by the risk factors, the presentation and the coags: a really prolonged bleeding time (PT, PTT).
  • Fibrinogen and the platelets used up in the clots.
  • A TEG, to examine the coagulopathy. Never interpreted.
  • Bleeding: the platelets and clotting factors are used up.
  • Ineffective peripheral tissue perfusion: microclots clog the small vessels of the hands and feet.
  • Microclots lodge: an organ dies when they clog its circulation. In septic shock, patients can lose hands and feet.
  • Clotting factors depleted: a really prolonged bleeding time.
  • Hypotension from low volume. Hypoxemia from fewer RBCs. Acidosis from tissue ischemia.
  • Goal: preserve organ function. Treat the underlying cause, such as sepsis or trauma.
  • Correct the hypotension, hypoxemia and acidosis.
  • Platelets first, the highest priority. FFP for fibrinogen and other clotting factors.
  • Heparin in very low doses blocks the clotting that started it. Counterintuitive.
  • Treat the pain. Monitor the labs very closely.
  • Often all at once: thrombophlebitis, pulmonary embolism, stroke, GI bleeding, renal failure.
The bleeding patient and anemia
  • Blood loss, the most common: post-op, trauma, a GI bleed.
  • Also RBC production (chronic kidney disease, radiation) and RBC destruction (autoimmune, the wrong blood transfused).
  • On a blood thinner: heparin, warfarin, apixaban, enoxaparin.
  • Tachycardia first. Pale, cool skin and mucous membranes. Loss of urine output.
  • Fatigue, SOB, angina, headache, lightheadedness, syncope, tachypnea.
  • Timing: a sudden loss shows every symptom quickly. A slow loss may sit up and talk with a hemoglobin of 4.
  • H&H and CBC, trended: deceptive in an acute bleed, diluted by fluid. The full extent shows after 48 to 72 hours.
  • Reticulocyte count, raised within several days.
  • Bleeding time: PT, PTT, fibrinogen. A TEG.
  • Deficient fluid volume: blood lost from the vessels.
  • Activity intolerance: too few RBCs carry oxygen. Getting from the bed to the chair uses all their energy.
  • Active bleeding leads to hypovolemic shock.
  • A coagulopathy develops quickly when actively bleeding, even on no anticoagulation. Cold makes it worse.
  • Identify the source and treat the cause: clip the bleed, reverse the thinner, the OR.
  • Transfuse: PRBCs primarily. A TEG shows what else. Fluid resuscitation as well.
  • Anticoagulants off. Weigh dressings. Limit invasive procedures.
  • Massive transfusion protocol for a patient bleeding to death: 1:1:1:1, warmed.
  • Acute kidney injury (renal): selective vasoconstriction hits the kidneys hard.
  • Angina, then high-output cardiac failure (cardiovascular).
  • Transfusion reactions, and TACO (respiratory).
Thrombocytopenia and HIT
  • Low platelets, technically under 150,000.
  • Usually a bone marrow issue: chemo, radiation, a bone marrow cancer. Also cardiac bypass, sepsis, cancers.
  • HIT: platelets trending down on a heparin drip.
  • Some antibiotics, antidysrhythmics such as amiodarone, and NSAIDs.
  • Skin: petechiae, purpura, ecchymosis.
  • Oozing mucous membranes: the nose, mouth and lips.
  • Bleeding in wounds, stool, urine, the nose and pulmonary secretions.
  • Temperature, when chemo or the bone marrow is the cause: with a very low white count, any temp is very concerning.
  • Platelet count: under 30,000 is critically low.
  • Serotonin release assay (SRA): tells whether it is HIT. Hard to obtain.
  • Coags, H&H and CBC monitored.
  • Bleeding: too few platelets for the platelet plug.
  • Infection, when chemo or the bone marrow is the cause: white blood cells decreased or not working, so no normal response to an infection.
  • Under 30,000: a risk for a spontaneous bleed.
  • A medication that lowers platelets drops the count further.
  • Find the cause and address it. HIT: stop the heparin. Dropping platelets: usually off anticoagulation.
  • Thrombocytopenic precautions: toothettes, electric shaver, no aspirin, no needle sticks where avoidable, protection from falls.
  • Meds evaluated with pharmacy. Platelets only when very low.
  • A head injury and a subarachnoid hemorrhage after a fall (neurologic).
The immunocompromised patient
  • WBCs decreased or non-functioning, congenital or acquired.
  • Organ transplant, bone marrow transplant, chemo, AIDS.
  • Usually asymptomatic until an infection. May not look very sick.
  • Temperature: with a very low white count, any temp is very concerning.
  • Opportunistic pneumonia: fever, chills, cough, dyspnea, fatigue.
  • CBC: the WBC count shows leukopenia.
  • CD4 counts, for a solid organ transplant or HIV and AIDS.
  • Infection: no normal white blood cell response.
  • Impaired gas exchange: an opportunistic pneumonia infects the lung parenchyma, and with no normal white cell response it can progress to respiratory distress, then respiratory failure.
  • Cannot mount a response to an infection.
  • Opportunistic infections. Infection and sepsis are the primary cause of death.
  • Protective isolation or neutropenic precautions: masks all the time, a HEPA filter room if needed.
  • No raw fruits and vegetables. Watch the temperature.
  • Teaching for home: wash fruits and vegetables, no raw fish or sushi, cook meat thoroughly.
  • Sepsis, which can set off DIC (hematologic).

The exemplars: DIC set off by sepsis from pneumonia, in the DIC tab. Anemia from blood loss after a hip replacement, in the Anemia from Blood Loss tab.

The Patient

Histories, Vital Signs and Medications

HPI: Raymond Ellis is a 72-year-old Black man brought to the ED by his wife at 1000. For 4 days he has had a cough bringing up thick, rust-colored sputum, with fever, chills and aching muscles. Since yesterday he has been short of breath walking across a room, and has a sharp pain in the right side of his chest when he breathes deeply or coughs. This morning his wife found him confused about the day and where he was. Last night she noticed his gums bled when he brushed his teeth, and a bruise where he bumped his arm on a door. Since last night his toes have ached with a burning pain, which he says is not like his usual leg cramps. He denies blood in his stool or urine, nosebleeds, vomiting and a recent fall. In the ED, blood cultures and a sputum culture were drawn, and antibiotics for his pneumonia were started at 1045, with 2 L of LR. Both IV sites oozed through their dressings. He came to the MICU at 1400.

PMH: Peripheral arterial disease, 6 years, with intermittent claudication after walking about a block. Hypertension, 20 years. Hyperlipidemia, 12 years.

SH: Married, lives with his wife in a one-story house. Retired electrician. Former smoker, 40 pack-years, quit 6 years ago when his PAD was diagnosed. Two beers a week. No drug use.

FH: Father died of a stroke at 74. Mother had hypertension.

Admission VS: BP 96/58 (MAP 71), HR 114 (sinus tachycardia), RR 28, shallow, SpO2 90% on room air and 94% on 2 L/min by nasal cannula, temperature 39.3 °C (102.7 °F), pain 5/10.

Home medications:

  • Aspirin, an antiplatelet, for peripheral arterial disease. Held since admission.
  • Lisinopril, for hypertension
  • Atorvastatin, for hyperlipidemia

Allergies: Sulfa (rash).

General Survey, Physical Exam, Labs and Diagnostics

  • General survey: 72-year-old man sitting up in bed, flushed and shivering under two blankets. Restless, picking at his gown. Speaks in short phrases of 4 or 5 words, stopping to cough. Oriented to person only, not to place or time: GCS 14 (E4, V4, M6). Anxious: "What's wrong with my feet?" Unshaven, in a hospital gown. His wife at the bedside.
  • Pain: 5/10 sharp pain in the right lower chest on deep breaths and coughing. A burning ache in his toes, 3/10, constant since last night.
  • Respiratory: RR 28, shallow, splinting his right side. Using his neck (sternocleidomastoid) muscles to breathe. Frequent cough, productive of thick, rust-colored sputum. Crackles in the right lower lobe, posterior and lateral. Breath sounds clear in the right upper and middle fields and the left anterior, posterior and lateral fields. SpO2 94% on 2 L/min by nasal cannula.
  • Cardiovascular: S1 and S2 present, rapid and regular. No murmur, gallop or rub. Telemetry: sinus tachycardia at 114. Neck veins flat at 45 degrees. No edema of the hands, sacrum, legs, ankles or feet bilaterally. Radial pulses 2+ and equal bilaterally. Dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial pulses 1+ bilaterally, weak, as his podiatrist always finds them. Capillary refill 2 seconds in the fingers, 4 seconds in the toes. Both feet cool to the ankles.
  • Integumentary: Skin hot, flushed and dry on the trunk. Petechiae across the chest and both forearms. A 6 cm ecchymosis at the left antecubital, where the ED drew blood. Oozing around both peripheral IV sites. Gums oozing blood along the lower teeth. The tips of 3 toes on the right foot and 2 on the left dusky purple and mottled, cool to touch. Fingertips pink.
  • Labs: CBC: WBC 19,800 with a shift to the left, hemoglobin 12.4, hematocrit 37%, platelets 72,000 (238,000 at his checkup 2 months ago). Coags: PT 18 seconds and PTT 46 seconds, both prolonged. Fibrinogen 140, low.
  • Diagnostic: Chest x-ray: infiltrates in the right lower lobe.

PartProblem 1Problem 2
Priority nursing problem
  • Bleeding
  • Ineffective peripheral tissue perfusion
Supporting cues
  • Platelets 72,000, down from 238,000 two months ago: being used up.
  • PT 18 and PTT 46, both prolonged, with fibrinogen 140, low: his clotting factors are being used up, and he takes longer to clot.
  • Oozing around both IV sites and from his gums: bleeding from insertion sites and mucous membranes.
  • Petechiae on his chest and arms, and an ecchymosis where blood was drawn: little bleeds under the skin.
  • The tips of 5 toes dusky purple, mottled and cool: little blood reaching them.
  • Capillary refill 4 seconds in the toes, against 2 in the fingers: the flow to his feet is slowest.
  • A new burning ache in his toes, 3/10, unlike his claudication: tissue short of blood.
  • Pedal pulses 1+ and feet cool to the ankles, with PAD: his feet started with less flow.
Relevance to the diagnosis
  • His pneumonia has become sepsis. Mediators from the bacteria and his white cells set off unregulated clotting: platelets clump, and microclots form throughout his small vessels. This exaggerated clotting uses up his platelets and clotting factors, fibrinogen among them, and his body, sensing the clots, tries to thin his blood. That is DIC, and it leaves him unable to clot: his bleeding time is prolonged, and any area that can bleed will bleed. He is oozing now. His aspirin, an antiplatelet, only adds to it, so it is held. Below 30,000, his platelets would put him at risk for a spontaneous bleed.
  • The same microclots that use up his clotting factors lodge in his small vessels and cut off the blood flow behind them: ischemia, and then necrosis. In sepsis, clots in the peripheral tissues can cost a patient their hands and feet, and DIC's skin signs often show first on the tips of the toes. His feet had little margin. Peripheral arterial disease means atherosclerosis in the arteries of his legs, and perfusion cannot get past it. Sepsis also opens his vessels wide, lowering his BP and the perfusion to his tissues. Dying tissue is very painful.
Concern rating
  • 2, urgent. His platelets are falling fast and he is already oozing. At 72,000, above the 30,000 of a spontaneous bleed, with a hemoglobin of 12.4, he is not expected to die within the hour.
  • 2, urgent. Without perfusion, his toes can die, and he could lose them. His fingertips are still pink and his MAP is 71: he is not expected to die within the hour.

PartAnswer
ComplicationA stroke (neurologic). At 0600 on his third day, found on morning rounds.
Why it could occurIn DIC, microclots lodge in the small vessels and clog an organ's circulation, and a stroke is one of the ways DIC shows. His brain had little margin: hypertension and hyperlipidemia have built atherosclerosis in his arteries, and large artery atherosclerosis is the biggest risk factor for an ischemic stroke.
Priority cuesHis right arm drifts down and he cannot lift it off the bed, his right leg is weak, and the right side of his face droops: weakness on one side. His speech is slurred, and he cannot find his words, though he follows commands: expressive aphasia. GCS 13 (E4, V3, M6), down from 15 last night. NIHSS 9, a moderate stroke. He was talking normally at 0400.
ActionsIndependent: the provider called at once, since time is brain cells. When he was last seen normal, 0400, reported, since the symptoms' onset matters above all. The NIHSS, GCS and level of consciousness checked and trended every hour. Dependent: a stat CT of the head without contrast, per order. It shows whether the stroke is hemorrhagic or ischemic, which decides what is done next. His shows no bleeding: an ischemic stroke, and neurology is called. No tPA: he is oozing, his platelets are 72,000, and his PT and PTT are prolonged. A clot buster is ruled out with active bleeding, platelets under 100,000, an INR over 1.7 or a prolonged PT or PTT. A thrombectomy can still pull the clot out.
EvaluationNIHSS: holding at 9 or trending down, which means his brain function is improving. A rising score means the stroke is extending.

Relevance to the diagnosis
  • She is bleeding from her new hip into her drain, her dressing and the tissue of her thigh, and blood lost from her vessels leaves too little volume to fill them. Her heart compensates first, with a faster rate. Her vessels clamp down to raise her BP and send what blood is left to her brain, heart and lungs. Her skin is restricted first, so it is pale and cool, and her kidneys next, so her urine output is falling. With the volume gone, her stroke volume cannot rise. Her hemoglobin may not show the whole loss: plasma is lost with the cells, and the liter of LR dilutes what is left. Active bleeding can lead to hypovolemic shock.
  • Her red cells carry oxygen on their hemoglobin, and with fewer of them, too little oxygen reaches her tissues, which survive on aerobic metabolism. At rest she makes up for it with a faster heart rate and breathing. Any effort asks for more oxygen than she can deliver, so standing or getting from the bed to the chair uses all her energy, and her brain goes lightheaded. She started with less. Her kidneys, damaged by chronic kidney disease, make less erythropoietin, so her marrow makes fewer red cells, and she was anemic before surgery. Making more red cells is the body's longer-term answer to anemia, and the one her kidneys cannot give.
Concern rating
  • 2, urgent. She is still bleeding, and her BP is holding only after a fluid bolus. With a MAP of 73, a hemoglobin of 8.0 and a GCS of 15, she is not expected to die within the hour.
  • 3, general. Effort is beyond her for now, but resting in bed, with her heart rate and breathing compensating, she is not in danger from it.

ComplicationAn acute kidney injury (renal). At 0700 the next morning. In the OR at 0030, a bleeding artery in her hip was tied off, and she has been stable since, her hemoglobin 8.2.
Why it could occurHer kidneys take about 20% of her cardiac output, so when she bled and her vessels clamped down to send blood to her brain, heart and lungs, her kidneys took a big hit. Hours of low BP last night cut their perfusion further, and after a stretch of hypotension and hypovolemia, a pre-renal AKI can develop within a day or two. Her kidneys had little margin, with chronic kidney disease.
Priority cuesUrine 10 to 15 mL/hr for 6 hours, under 0.5 mL/kg/hr for her 70 kg, though her MAP is back to 72: oliguria. Creatinine 2.4, up from her usual 1.5, and BUN 46: azotemia, and the creatinine is the most reliable sign of it. Potassium 5.6, up from 4.4: her kidneys are not excreting it.
ActionsIndependent: urine output measured every hour, and her telemetry watched for a peaked T wave until her potassium is back in range. Dependent: IV fluid replacement per order, normal saline or LR, to flush her kidneys, while she can tolerate it, with her electrolytes monitored. Interdisciplinary: a team talk with pharmacy, every drug on her list checked so that nothing nephrotoxic reaches kidneys this poorly perfused, switched or lowered where needed.
EvaluationUrine output: back above 0.5 mL/kg/hr, about 35 mL/hr for her, and her creatinine trending back toward 1.5 over the next days: her kidneys are recovering. A creatinine still rising, or a potassium climbing, means the AKI is worsening.
