TBI with increased ICP
  • An external impact to the head. Falls the leading cause, then struck by or against, motor vehicle crashes, assaults.
  • Highest risk: males, children under 4, young adults 15 to 19.
  • If awake: a significant headache, vomiting, blurred vision, lethargy, changes in behavior.
  • A falling GCS. One pupil a different size: pressure on one side of the brain.
  • Late: Cushing's triad, bradycardia, irregular respirations and a widened pulse pressure.
  • ICP above 20 on an ICP monitor, usually placed for a GCS of 3 to 8. CPP = MAP − ICP, the goal above 70.
  • Urine output and sodium, for DI, SIADH and cerebral salt wasting.
  • PaO2: below 50 increases ICP.
  • Ineffective cerebral tissue perfusion: swelling in a skull that cannot give raises the ICP and lowers the CPP.
  • Ineffective airway clearance: a falling GCS. Can the patient maintain the airway?
  • Seizures: a higher risk with any TBI.
  • Secondary injury: ischemia, inflammation, swelling, hematomas. Decreased cerebral perfusion is the primary driver.
  • Herniation: downward the most damaging. Recovery very unlikely. Then brain death.
  • Airway and oxygenation: the priority.
  • ICP below 20, CPP above 70: head of the bed at least 30°, head midline, no fever, mannitol, sedation.
  • Surgery: burr holes for a subdural hematoma, a craniectomy.
  • Endocrine: DI, SIADH, cerebral salt wasting. The pituitary is damaged. Monitor urine output.
  • Hypermetabolism: higher nutritional needs.
Stroke
  • Ischemic, 87%: large artery atherosclerosis, the biggest risk factor. Cardioembolic, as from AFib. Lacunar.
  • Hemorrhagic: uncontrolled hypertension the most common cause. A ruptured aneurysm, an AV malformation.
  • A TIA before: a warning.
  • Sudden weakness or numbness of one side, on the side opposite the stroke. Slurred speech, a visual disturbance, dizziness.
  • A sudden-onset severe headache: "worst headache of my life".
  • The time of onset: critical information.
  • A stat CT without contrast: hemorrhagic or ischemic, which decides what is done next.
  • The NIHSS: the higher the score, the more severe the stroke. Trended with the GCS.
  • Blood glucose: hypoglycemia can mimic a stroke.
  • Ineffective cerebral tissue perfusion: brain cells survive 3 to 4 minutes without oxygen. The penumbra can still be saved.
  • Aspiration: swallowing and the gag reflex impaired. A suspected stroke is NPO.
  • Bleeding after tPA.
  • Many worsen in the first 24 to 48 hours.
  • The dying cells, and secondary cerebral edema. A severe stroke raises the ICP.
  • Hemorrhagic: a very poor prognosis.
  • Ischemic: bust the clot. tPA first-line, within 4.5 hours of onset. A thrombectomy through interventional radiology.
  • Hemorrhagic: BP control and surgery.
  • Once stable: anticoagulants, antiplatelets, statins.
  • Bleeding after tPA: anyone who gets it is at high risk.
  • Aspiration pneumonia: impaired swallowing and a decreased level of consciousness.
  • Airway, breathing and circulation, in a severe stroke.
Spinal cord injury
  • Most common at ages 16 to 30. Motor vehicle accidents, falls, violence, sports, diving.
  • Often with a TBI.
  • Spinal shock: complete loss of motor, sensory and reflex activity below the injury.
  • Neurogenic shock: hypotension, bradycardia, hypothermia. More likely at T6 or higher.
  • The level decides the loss: C4 and C6 tetraplegia, T6 and L1 paraplegia.
  • None taught.
  • Ineffective breathing pattern: a cervical or thoracic injury can affect the diaphragm.
  • Ineffective tissue perfusion: neurogenic shock lowers the BP, and the cord needs a MAP of 85 to 90.
  • Pressure ulcers: no movement or sensation, impaired circulation.
  • Cord edema can ascend or descend, then microscopic hemorrhages, ischemia and axonal degeneration: a permanent loss.
  • Neurogenic shock. The permanence is not known until spinal shock resolves.
  • Airway first: a paralyzed diaphragm needs intubation.
  • MAP 85 to 90 for about 7 days: no systolic hypotension.
  • Immobilized until cleared. Unstable fracture: surgery within 24 hours.
  • GI: paralytic ileus. GU: urinary retention. Spinal shock leaves the bowel and bladder with atony.
  • DVT and PE: venous stasis. Pressure ulcers.
  • Later, at T6 or above: autonomic dysreflexia, an elevated, uncontrolled BP.
Meningitis
  • A nearby local infection: an open skull fracture, an abscess on the skull or in the brain, an eye abscess or infection.
  • A basilar skull fracture leads to it more quickly.
  • Headache, stiff neck and vomiting, over 1 to 2 days.
  • Fever, a throbbing headache, nuchal rigidity.
  • Spinal tap: WBCs in the CSF indicate bacterial meningitis. There should be none.
  • Hyperthermia: infection of the meninges and the CSF.
  • Ineffective cerebral tissue perfusion: the inflammation can cause cerebral edema and increased ICP.
  • Cerebral edema and increased ICP from the inflammation.
  • Bacterial: a very high risk of neurologic problems.
  • Bacterial: IV antibiotics STAT, a neurological emergency.
  • Droplet precautions, seizure precautions, temperature management, ICP monitoring.
  • None taught.
Seizures
  • A complication of TBI, stroke, really any brain damage. After a TBI, blood on the brain irritates the tissue.
  • A decreased neurologic status with no clear cause.
  • Status epilepticus: a seizure longer than 5 minutes, or repeated seizures over 30 minutes.
  • EEG monitoring, continuous or a snapshot: brain activity and seizure activity.
  • Labs: none taught.
  • Injury: seizure precautions, nothing forced into the mouth, no restraint.
  • Ineffective airway clearance: maintain the airway, during and after the seizure.
  • Seizures greatly increase ICP.
  • Status epilepticus: a medical emergency. Over 10 minutes can cause death.
  • Seizure precautions: suction, oxygen, padded rails, an Ambu bag.
  • IV benzodiazepines for status epilepticus: lorazepam or diazepam. IV access kept.
  • Levetiracetam: continuously, preventatively, or as treatment.
  • None taught.

The Patient

Histories, Vital Signs and Medications

HPI: Luis Ortega is a 44-year-old Hispanic man brought to the ED by ambulance at 1640. At 1600 he was on a ladder cleaning his gutters when his wife saw him stiffen and jerk for about a minute, then fall about 10 feet onto the concrete driveway, landing on the back of his head. He did not respond for a few minutes, then woke confused and vomited. He vomited again in the ambulance. He reports a pounding headache. His wife says he takes his seizure medication every day, and his last seizure was 8 months ago. He bit his tongue and was incontinent of urine during the seizure. He denies neck or back pain, and no other injury is found. No fluid has come from his nose or ears.

PMH: Epilepsy (a seizure disorder) since age 24, controlled on medication. Hypertension, 5 years. GERD, 3 years.

SH: Married, two teenage children, lives in a two-story house. Electrician. Never smoked. One or two beers on weekends. No drug use.

FH: Father has hypertension. Mother has type 2 diabetes.

Admission VS: BP 156/88, HR 66 (normal sinus rhythm), RR 18, SpO2 95% on room air, temperature 37.2 °C (99.0 °F), pain 6/10.

Home medications:

  • Levetiracetam, for seizures
  • Lisinopril, for hypertension
  • Pantoprazole, for GERD

Allergies: Codeine (nausea and vomiting).

General Survey, Physical Exam, Labs and Diagnostics

  • General survey: 44-year-old man lying on the stretcher with his eyes closed. Opens his eyes when his name is said, then drifts off. Confused: knows his name, not where he is or the date. Restless, pulling at his blanket. A 4 cm laceration at the back of his scalp, closed with staples in the ED, his hair matted with dried blood. A bitten tongue. In a hospital gown, his wet clothes removed. His wife at the bedside, tearful. GCS 12.
  • Pain: 6/10 pounding headache at the back of his head and behind his eyes, constant since the fall.
  • Neurologic: GCS 12: eyes open to speech (3), confused (4), localizes pain (5). Pushes the examiner's hand away from a nail bed squeeze, but does not follow commands. Pupils equal, 3 mm, round and brisk to light. Moves all four limbs, strength equal bilaterally. Face symmetrical. No clear fluid from the nose or ears. Gag reflex present.
  • Respiratory: RR 18, regular and unlabored. No use of the neck or thoracic accessory muscles. Breath sounds clear in the anterior, posterior and lateral fields bilaterally. Infrequent, nonproductive cough.
  • Cardiovascular: S1 and S2 present and regular, no murmur. Monitor: normal sinus rhythm at 66. No JVD at 30 degrees. No edema of the hands, legs, ankles or feet bilaterally. Radial and pedal pulses 2+ and equal bilaterally. Capillary refill under 3 seconds in fingers and toes. Skin warm and dry.
  • Labs: BMP: sodium 139, potassium 4.0, chloride 103, CO2 25, BUN 14, creatinine 0.9, glucose 108. Serum osmolality 286, normal (270 to 290).
  • Diagnostic: CT of the head: a small subdural hematoma over the right side of the brain, and a contusion at the front, opposite the impact. Mild cerebral edema. No skull fracture.

Relevance to the diagnosis
  • His fall was an acceleration and deceleration injury. The back of his head struck the concrete, and his brain rebounded into the front of his skull: two impacts, two areas of the brain. The contusion is the primary injury. The hematoma and the swelling are the secondary injury, the brain's biochemical response, and they keep growing. Brain tissue, blood and CSF share a skull that cannot give, so as they expand, his ICP rises. CPP is the MAP minus the ICP: a rising ICP leaves less blood reaching his brain, and decreased cerebral perfusion is the primary driver of more secondary injury.
  • His seizure disorder already makes him prone to seizures, and a seizure caused today's fall. Any TBI raises the risk further: blood on the brain irritates the tissue. A seizure now would greatly increase his ICP and his brain's metabolic demand, worsening the injury, and could cost him his airway. He needs seizure precautions and his anti-seizure medication.
Concern rating
  • 2, urgent. His ICP is rising and needs neuro checks every hour. His pupils are equal and brisk, he localizes, and his vital signs show no Cushing's triad: he is not expected to die within the hour.
  • 2, urgent. A seizure would raise his ICP and threaten his airway, so the precautions must be in place quickly. He has not seized since the fall.

Early the Next Morning

Mr. Ortega came to the neuro ICU at 1900. He was kept with the head of the bed at 30 degrees and his head midline, with neuro checks every hour. He was placed on seizure precautions, with suction, oxygen, padded rails and an Ambu bag in the room, and given levetiracetam, to prevent seizures. At 0300 his nurse can no longer wake him with his name. He opens his eyes only to a nail bed squeeze and moans without words, and both arms curl in to his chest: decorticate posturing. His right pupil is 6 mm and sluggish, his left 3 mm and brisk. He vomits again. His heart rate has slowed, his BP has climbed with a widening gap, and his breathing is slow and irregular.

Updated VS: BP 184/74, HR 52 (sinus bradycardia), RR 10 and irregular, SpO2 92% on room air, temperature 37.4 °C (99.3 °F), pain unable to rate: grimaces to a nail bed squeeze. GCS 7.

A Complication in Another System

PartAnswer
ComplicationDiabetes insipidus (endocrine). The afternoon of his second day, about 36 hours after the fall.
Why it could occurADH is released from the posterior pituitary, in the brain, and head trauma can damage it: the cause of neurogenic DI. With too little ADH, his kidneys reabsorb less water, and he loses pure water. He becomes dehydrated and hemoconcentrated, and his sodium rises. After head trauma it comes on abruptly, with the most severe signs.
Priority cuesUrine output 450, 520 and 480 mL in the last 3 hours, as pale as water. Urine specific gravity 1.002: very dilute. Serum sodium 151, up from 139: hypernatremia. Serum osmolality 309, up from 286. BP 108/62 and HR 106 as his volume falls. His last mannitol was 36 hours ago.
ActionsDependent: desmopressin per order, a synthetic ADH, so his kidneys hold water again, with IV fluids per order to replace what he has lost. Independent: strict intake and output, the urine output and specific gravity every hour, and a daily weight. The provider is told at once if the urine volume rises while the specific gravity falls: he is getting worse. Neuro checks continue, because a shifting sodium shrinks and swells brain cells.
EvaluationUrine output: falling within hours of the desmopressin, with the specific gravity rising toward 1.010 to 1.030.

The Patient

The Patient
